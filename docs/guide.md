# A guide to Glaukopis

Glaukopis is where a text is written, of whatever kind: a book, an article,
a thesis, a novel, a play, a report, a letter. The work is done in one place:
working out what you think, collecting what you have read, writing, and
producing the finished text in the form it is to be published in.

It has two places, reached from the left edge of the window: the **projects**,
one for each piece of writing, and the **library**, which holds your
references and is the same for all projects.

## The idea

You do not write a document in Glaukopis. You make a **map** of your ideas,
and write into it, and the map *is* the document.

Every idea is an **element** of the project. An element has a name and may
have text. Elements stand under one another: that is the order of the
argument. They may also be *associated* across the project, where one idea
bears on another without belonging under it. A project is one tree, and at
its top stands the project itself, an element like the others: its name is
the project's name, and its text is what comes before everything else.

Any element can be looked at in three ways, with all that is under it, and
it is the same element in all of them:

| View | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Diagram** | Seeing the whole, and moving ideas about. The element stands at the centre, and what is under it about it: this is its map. |
| **Text** | Writing. The name of the element is the title, and the names of those under it are the headings. |
| **Preview** | Seeing the manuscript as it will be sent. |

So there is no moment at which the outline is put away and the writing
begins. An element with a name and nothing else is a thought to come back to;
one with three paragraphs and citations is a section that is nearly done.
Both are in the same tree. A thought that has no place yet is a tree of its
own under the project, until it is moved to where it belongs; and any
element, a chapter or the whole book, can be entered, to be seen alone and
printed as a document of its own (see *One tree, entered anywhere*).

## A first project

1. Choose **Begin a project** and give it the working title of the book or
   article. It opens on its text, a sheet with one heading, named after the
   project: a new project begins with writing. Write under the heading, or
   press **Ctrl+D** for the diagram, a map with one element, the project
   itself.
2. In the diagram, press **Tab**. A new element appears under the project:
   type its name and press **Enter**.
3. Press **Enter** again for one beside it, **Tab** for one under it.
4. Double-click an element to write its text.

Everything is saved as you work. There is nothing to press.

To go from one project to another without the home page, open **the list of
projects** at the left of a project, with the button at the left of the bar
over the map or **Ctrl+Shift+L**. It is the list of the home page, with its
folders and its search; a click opens a project, and the one that is open is
marked. Under the open project stands its whole tree, folded but for the way
down to the element in view, which is marked; a click on an element enters
it (see *One tree, entered anywhere*). The list stays open from project to
project until you close it, and its edge can be dragged to make it wider.

### Many projects

The page of projects has two forms, chosen at its head and remembered.
**Last used** shows the twelve projects last worked on, as cards. **All
projects** is a line for each, in folders: make a folder with **New folder**,
or inside another from that folder's menu; drag a project onto a folder, or
choose **Move to folder** in its menu, and **Out of folders** to take it out
again. A folder shows how many projects it holds, and is opened and closed
with a click; a double click shows it as a map. Deleting a folder keeps what
is in it, which moves up to where the folder was. The folders are kept on
this computer alone, not in the projects: a project you share is in your
folders, not in the others'. The field above the list finds a project by its
name. Folders and projects stand in the order of their names, here and on
the map.

Every change to folders and projects, wherever it is made, says itself in a
message at the foot of the window with **Undo**: a name, a move, a new
folder or project, a deletion. A project deleted is brought back from the
trash, and a folder deleted is made again with what was in it. On the map,
**Ctrl+Z** and **Ctrl+Shift+Z** undo and redo them, one after another, as
long as Glaukopis runs.

### The map of the projects

Above every project stands the map of all of them: the owl at the centre,
the folders as plain names, nested as they are, and the projects under
them, each drawn as the top element of its own map, those in no folder
hanging from the owl itself. It is reached
from the owl at the top of the list of projects, and from the top of any
project with **All projects** at the left of the bar, or **Alt+Shift+Up**,
which comes to the folder the project is in, with the project selected. The
map is moved by dragging its ground and turned larger and smaller as the
diagram is. A project you share has a sign of people beside its name, and
one you joined a sign of a link, here, in the list and in the bar over the
map.

It is worked as a project's diagram is, in a look of its own:

- **Selecting.** A click selects a folder or a project, ringed in the colour
  of the buttons; **Ctrl** and a click add one or take it away, **Shift**
  adds, **Ctrl+A** selects all, **Escape** lets go. The arrows go from one to
  the next, and with **Shift** select more.
- **Opening.** A double click on a card, the small button at its corner, or
  **Alt+Shift+Down** opens the project. The same on a folder shows the folder
  at the centre, with what is in it; the bar says what holds it, and
  **Alt+Shift+Up**, the button at its corner or a name in the bar goes up.
- **Making.** **Enter** makes a new project and **Tab** a new folder, in the
  folder selected, beside the project selected, or at the top; its name is
  written where it stands, and **Enter** keeps it. **Escape**, or no name,
  and nothing is made. The menu of a folder and of the map's ground has the
  same, as **New project here** and **New folder here**.
- **Renaming.** **F2** opens the name of what is selected for writing; a
  letter typed begins a new name; **Escape** keeps the old one.
- **Moving.** **Alt** and the arrow that points away from the owl put what
  is selected into the folder before it; the arrow towards the owl takes it
  out of the folder it is in. Or drag it: onto a folder, into it; onto a
  card, into that card's folder; onto the owl, out of all folders. What is
  dragged from the map can be dropped on the list, and the other way.
- **Placing.** A folder or a project dragged onto the ground of the map
  stays where it is let go, and nothing else moves; the places are kept on
  this computer. **Place everything by itself**, in the map's menu, puts
  them back in order. Dropped on a folder, it goes into it; on the owl, out
  of all folders.
- **Folding.** **Space**, or the sign before a folder's name, hides the
  projects in it or shows them. A folder of many projects begins folded.
- **The maps of the projects.** A project stands on the map as the top
  element of its own map stands in it, and opens as an element does: the
  small button at its side, with the number of what is folded away, or
  **Space** with the project selected, shows its trees under it, each
  folded; every unfolding shows one more level. A double click on an
  element, the button at its corner or **Alt+Shift+Down** opens the project
  there. The elements are moved as in the project's own map: dragged onto
  the ground they stand where they are let go, onto another element they go
  under it, onto another project they are carried there; **Alt** and the
  arrows move them up and down, under the one before, and out. Each move is
  made in the project itself, and **Ctrl+Z** undoes it. Renaming, making
  and deleting elements is done in the project.
- **Deleting.** **Delete** deletes what is selected, after asking: a
  project goes to the trash, and what is in a folder moves up.

**Show all of it**, at the foot of the map, or **Ctrl+0**, shows all of the
map at once, however far apart what is on it stands.

**Shift+F10**, or a right click, opens the menu of what is selected, and the
keyboard in the rail lists all of these keys under *On the map of the
projects*.

In the list at the left of a project, a project that is not open can be
unfolded too: its trees are read from the disk and shown, and a click on one
of them opens the project there. **Open beside**, in the menu of another
project or of one of its elements, shows it beside the project you are in.

**A map of the projects…**, in the menu at the head of the page, makes a new
project of the projects themselves: the folders as elements, nested as the
folders are, and under each the projects in it, with their descriptions as
text. With **everything in them**, each project has its whole tree of
elements under it, names and texts, so that all your work can be seen and
searched as one map. The citations keep their references; a cross-reference
points at nothing in the new project, and comments stay behind. The new
project opens on its text.

The element in view stands at the centre of the map, never folded, and what

Lines between an element and what is under it are straight. **Associations**
are curved. To make one, right-click an element, choose **Associate with…**,
and click the other element; or, from the keys, **Shift+F10**, **Associate
with…**, the arrows to the other element, and **Enter**. An association can be given a few words that say
what it is.

An element that has text shows a small mark. The box in which the text is
written can be dragged by the room around its name to wherever it is wanted,
and the next box opened opens there.

**How far the writing has come.** Right-click an element, **Status**, and say
whether it is an idea, a draft or done; for several at once, select them
first. It is shown lightly beside the name, as an empty ring, half a dot or a
whole one, with the words of a draft or of what is done. The foot of the
diagram then says how far the map has come, as *2 done · 3 drafts · 1 idea ·
4,210 words written*, and the foot of the text says it too.

## The text

**Ctrl+D** turns between the diagram and the text. In the text, the element
in view is the title, and each element under it is a section: its name is
the heading, and the depth of the heading is how deep the element stands
under the one in view. Of a project entered at its top, as a new one is, the
project's name is the title, and its text comes before the first section.
When the element in view has no text yet, the cursor is in its text as it
is shown, and what is typed goes there; an empty text is a line high, and a
press on it, or beside it, puts the cursor in it.
How deep each element stands is shown in the margin beside its heading, on
the screen only: by its number, as in an outline (1, 1.2, 1.2.3, counting
only the names printed as headings), by a dot for each level, or by setting
each level further in, with a faint line down from the element it is under.
Choose which under **Document structure** in the settings, where **Editor
font** chooses the font your text is set in while you write, and the size
beside it how large; neither touches the preview or what is exported.

Write as in any editor. The tools are over the text: the kind of the
paragraph the cursor is in, a button that opens the kinds (text, quotation,
list, numbered list, and the rest under **More…**); italics, bold and small
capitals, and **Words**, which has the other marks and the kinds of words;
**Cite**, **Note** and **Insert**, which has pictures, tables and
mathematics; and **Comment**. They act where the cursor is, or on what is
selected. The same tools are in the box that opens when an element is
double-clicked in the diagram. Before them, over the text, stands
**New element**, which adds an element after the one the cursor is in, or
under it; and at their end a button that turns the checking of spelling off
and on.

Everything the tools do can be done from the keys, and much of it by typing:

| To | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Cite a reference | **Cite**, or type **@**, and begin to type an author or a title |
| Give the page | Type it when the citation has been chosen: `73`, `73–75` |
| Write a note | **Note**, or **Ctrl+Alt+F** |
| Put in a picture | **Insert**, or **Ctrl+Alt+P**; or drop the file on the text; or paste |
| Write a formula in the line | **Insert**, or **Ctrl+Alt+M** |
| Write an equation on a line of its own | **Insert**, or **Ctrl+Alt+E** |
| Set words in italics or bold | The tools, or **Ctrl+I**, **Ctrl+B** |
| Underline words | **Words**, or **Ctrl+U** |
| Choose the kind of a paragraph, or make a kind of your own | The kind button; **Make a kind…** under **More…** in its menu |
| Begin a quotation or a list | Type `> `, `- ` or `1. ` at the start of a line |
| Make a dash | Type `--` for –, `---` for — |
| Divide an element in two | Right-click where it is to be divided: **Split here**; or **Ctrl+Enter** |
| Add an element after the one the cursor is in | **Ctrl+Enter**; in a text, what follows the cursor goes into it |
| Add an element under it, the first, right after its text | **Ctrl+Shift+Enter**; in a text, what follows the cursor goes into it |
| Make the element deeper or less deep | **Alt+Right**, **Alt+Left**; or **Tab**, **Shift+Tab** in its name |
| Move the element up or down among those beside it | **Alt+Up**, **Alt+Down**; the cursor stays where it is |

| Move an element to another place | Drag it by the grip beside its heading: the line shows where it goes, and how far to the right the pointer is says how deep, under the element above, after it, or as far out as before the element below |

A text has two breaks: **Enter** begins a new paragraph, and
**Shift+Enter** breaks the line within the paragraph, as in an address or a
title of two lines. A break within a paragraph is shown by a faint **↵** at
the end of its line, on the screen only: it is not printed, copied or
searched. The button with the sign, beside that of spelling at the end of the
writing tools, hides it and shows it again, everywhere.

Text pasted as text keeps every line break: a lone one as a break within
the paragraph, an empty line between paragraphs, and each further empty line
as an empty paragraph.

What a sign has done is undone by **Backspace**, should the sign have been
meant as a sign.

Notes, citations, formulas, figures and cross-references each have a box of
their own, which opens when they are pressed. The arrows go through the
text without opening them: what the cursor comes to is selected, and
**Enter** opens it. **Backspace** at the beginning of a paragraph selects a
figure or a table that stands before it, and a second **Backspace** takes it
away.

Associations are shown as narrow lines in the left margin, between the
sections they join. Click one to go to either end of it, to give it a few
words, or to remove it.

### The outline

**Ctrl+Shift+O**, or the button at the left of the bar over the map, shows
the outline of what is in view beside whatever view is open, diagram, text
or timeline: every element by its name, as deep as it stands, and in
the text the one at the top marked; the line beside it can be dragged.
Press a name to go there; what it is folded under is opened. An entry with
something under it has a chevron, which folds that away in the outline and
opens it again, as the left and right arrows do; this folding is the
outline's own and changes nothing in the text. The up and down arrows go
from name to name, and **Alt** with the arrows moves an element, as in the
text.

### Folding elements away

A long text is easier to work in when what you are not working on is out of
the way. Every element that has text, or other elements under it, has a
small arrow beside its name. Press it, and the element is folded away: its
name stays, and its own text and all that is under it are hidden. A line
under the name says how much that is. Press the arrow or the line to open
it again. **Ctrl+Alt+U** does the same for the element the cursor is in.

Each element is folded by itself. What was folded under an element is
folded still when the element over it is folded and opened again, so the
text stays as you arranged it. To open an element with everything under
it at once, however deep:

- **Open all**, in the line that says what is folded away; or
- press the arrow with **Shift** held down; or
- **Ctrl+Alt+Shift+U**; or
- **Open all that is folded under it**, in the menu of the element. From
  the title of the document, that opens the whole text.

**Fold away all under it**, in the same menu, does the opposite: the
element stays open, and all under it is folded. From the title, that leaves
the parts of the document by their names, to be opened one at a time.

The name of a folded element can be changed as any other. **Enter** in the
name opens the element, and the cursor goes into its text; the arrows up
and down go past a text that is folded away.

What is folded away is remembered with the project on this computer, and is
as you left it when you open the project again. It is about what you see,
and not about the document: nothing is folded for those you share the
project with, the diagram is not changed by it, and the preview and the
documents that are made hold the whole text. The words counted under the
text are those of the whole text as well.

When something takes you to an element that is folded away, as a cross-reference,
an association or a new element under a folded one, what it is under is
opened.

### Notes

A note is written in a small panel that opens where the note stands, and is
shown in the text as its number. In the text the notes are numbered through
all that is in view, as they will be in the document. An element that is
left out of the document numbers its own.

Whether notes stand at the foot of the page or at the end of the text is
said by the layout of the document. Some books keep two kinds of notes apart: the
author's remarks at the foot of the page, say, and the sources at the end.
For that, a single note can be set to stand at the other place, in the panel
it is written in. Such notes are lettered, *a*, *b*, *c*, in the text and in
the document, so that they are told from the numbered ones.

### Figures

A *picture* is a file: a photograph, a drawing. A *figure* is a picture as
it stands in a text, with what is said of it there, its caption, and its
number in the document. The same picture can be a figure in many texts.

Put a figure in with **Insert**, from a file or from the store of pictures;
by dropping a file on the text where it is to stand; by pasting a picture
that was copied; or by dragging a picture from the pictures beside the map
(see *Pictures* below). A picture dropped
on an element in the diagram becomes a figure at the end of its text.

What is said of the figure is written under the picture, where the cursor is
when the figure has been put in. It can hold citations and formulas. **Enter**
leaves the figure, and the writing goes on under it.

Pressing the picture opens the rest that can be said of it:

- **How wide it is**, as a share of the width of the text in the document.
- **Where it stands**: to the left, in the middle or to the right; and, of a
  figure at a side, whether **the text flows around it**. See *Where things
  stand* below.
- **What it shows**, in words, for those who cannot see it. This goes into
  the documents that can hold it.
- Whether it is **numbered**. The figures are numbered through the document:
  the number shown where you write is the one the figure has there.
- **Keep the caption with the picture**, so that figures made with the same
  picture later begin with the same words; and **Use the picture's own**,
  which says of this figure what is kept with the picture.
- **Another picture** in its place, or the figure removed.

The word before the number, where what is said of the figure stands and how
it is set, are said by the layout of the document: *Figure 1.* under the
picture in one, **Figure 1** over it in another. A layout can also have the figures
gathered at the end of the document, as many journals ask of a manuscript;
a line in the text then says where each belongs.

PNG, JPEG and SVG are kept as they are. GIF, WebP, TIFF and BMP are made
into PNG when they are put in, since not every kind of document can hold
them. One picture may hold 50 MB.

### Tables

Put a table in with **Insert › Table…**: point at how many rows and columns
it is to have, or write the numbers, and say whether the first row is
headings. The cursor then stands in the first cell.

- **Tab** goes to the next cell and **Shift+Tab** to the one before; **Tab**
  in the last cell makes a new row. The arrows go from cell to cell where a
  cell ends.
- What is said of the table, its caption, is written over it. It can hold
  citations and formulas, as a cell can. **Enter** there leaves the table.
- While the cursor is in the table, a small bar stands over it: rows and
  columns are added and removed, cells joined and split, the first row and
  the first column made headings, and what the cells hold set to the left,
  in the middle or to the right. Drag over cells to select several. The
  same is in the menu that opens by the right button of the mouse.
- **Table** in the bar opens what can be said of the whole table: how wide
  it is, as wide as it needs or a share of the width of the text; whether
  it is numbered; where it stands and whether the text flows around it
  (see *Where things stand* below); and the table removed.
- **Backspace** at the beginning of a table in which nothing is written
  takes the table away.

The tables are numbered through the document, as the figures are, and can be
pointed to. The word before the number, whether what is said of the table
stands over or under it, the lines of the table and the size of its type are
said by the layout of the document: most have a line over the table, one under its
headings and one under the table, and no lines between the columns.

A table that was copied, from a web page or a document, becomes a table
when it is pasted into the text. Rows copied from a spreadsheet and pasted
into a cell fill the cells from there, and the table grows as it must.

#### Tables from files

**Insert › Table from a file…**, or a file dropped on the text where the
table is to stand, brings in a table that was made elsewhere. A file
dropped on an element in the diagram becomes a table at the end of its text.

| Kind of file | Endings |
| --- | --- |
| Values parted by commas, semicolons or tabs | `.csv`, `.tsv`, `.tab`, `.txt` |
| OpenDocument spreadsheet | `.ods` |
| Excel | `.xlsx`, `.xlsm`, `.xlsb`, `.xls` |

You are shown the first rows as they will be, and say which sheet it is to
be where the file has several, whether the first row and the first column
are headings, and what is said of the table. Columns that hold numbers are
set to the right.

What is brought in is what the cells hold, as text: a formula comes as what
it gave, a date as `2024-03-01`. How a cell was set to be shown is not
read, so a share shown as 25 % comes as 0.25. A table is a copy: it does not
follow the file when the file is changed. One table may have 2000 rows and
100 columns.

### Lines and paragraphs, verse, and texts side by side

A text is paragraphs, and a paragraph is lines. The keys are the same in
every kind of text:

| Key | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Enter** | A new paragraph of the same kind, with the same indent and numbering. On an empty paragraph of a kind, the paragraph becomes text: so one leaves a list, a verse or a dialogue. |
| **Shift+Enter** | A new line within the paragraph. |
| **Backspace** at the start of a line | Joins it to the line before; at the start of a paragraph, to the paragraph before, whose kind holds. |
| **Tab**, **Shift+Tab** | Indents a line of verse, or any other paragraph, a step more or less. |

Nothing else changes lines or paragraphs: changing the kind of a paragraph
changes its kind and nothing more.

A poem is written as it is written on paper: a line of verse ends with
**Shift+Enter**, and a stanza with **Enter**. Choose **Verse** as the kind of
its paragraphs, and each paragraph is a stanza and each of its lines a line.
Enter within a line parts the stanza there, the line break becoming the break
between the two. Tab indents the line the cursor is in a step, for the
shorter lines of lyric, and Shift+Tab takes it back; over several lines
selected, each of them. A **Speaker** or a **Stage direction** is a paragraph
of its own kind among the lines, and does not part them from each other; the
same two are kinds of words as well, under **Words**, for a name or a
direction within a line, as editions set them.

Numbers belong to what they number. **Numbers**, the **#** button among
the tools, offers the three things that number, each saying what it
numbers: **Number these lines…**, the lines you write; **Reference mark…**,
a place in prose (see *Reference marks*); and **Number the lines of the
page**, every line of the printed page, numbered in the margin, which is
marked when it is on and is said of the document of the element in view and
of all under it, over what its layout says.

**Number these lines…** numbers the paragraphs selected, or, where nothing
is selected, the paragraphs of the cursor's kind standing together with the
speakers and directions among them: a verse with its speakers, a run of
quoted paragraphs, the paragraphs of prose around the cursor. Those
paragraphs are then a **numbered stretch**, and one count runs through all
of it, whatever the kinds of its paragraphs: through speakers and
directions, which are not counted, across stanzas, through prose, in which a
paragraph is one line. A stretch is what its paragraphs say: paragraphs
numbered alike, standing together, are counted as one. Where the cursor is
in a numbered stretch already, the same window changes how it is numbered.
**Numbers…** in the menu of a bracket does the same for the paragraphs it is
beside (see *Kinds of paragraph and of words*).

In its window, **Numbering** is one of three: **Not numbered**; **Counted
from the first number given**, which counts the lines and begins where you
gave a number; or **Only the numbers given**, which shows the numbers you
gave and counts nothing. **Number from** gives the first counted line its
number, 45 for lines that begin at line 45; paragraphs of prose not yet
numbered are counted from 1, every one. **Show a number every** and the side
the numbers stand on are the layout's unless you say otherwise here: every
fifth line, at the left, in the layouts that ship. An empty line is never
counted. The numbers stand in the margin, in the text and in the document;
in Word and Writer they stand at the head of their lines, small.

The same window says what is to be said of the line the cursor is in, for
editions whose numbers do not follow the lines as they are set, as those of
Greek drama do not:

- **Its number or label**: *1234*, *567a*, *1094a1*. It is always shown, as
  dark as the text where you write, so that you see which numbers you gave;
  and the count goes on from its last number: after *1234* comes 1235, after
  *1094a1* comes *1094a2*, and after *567a* comes 568. A label without a
  number, such as *\**, is shown and leaves the count as it was.
- **Not counted**: the count passes over the line, as over the second half
  of a line shared between two speakers, or a line the edition does not
  count. With a label of its own it shows the label, and nothing otherwise.
- **Counted, its number not shown**: the count goes on through the line as
  through any other, but no number stands beside it in the document, even
  where one would fall, as where a number would crowd a short line. Where
  you write, the number it holds back stands faint in parentheses, so that
  you see it is counted.
- **Words in the margin**: a note of the metre, the number another edition
  gives, anything to stand beside the line. They are not counted. They
  stand in the margin across from the numbers, and in Word and Writer at
  the end of their line, small.

What is said of a line stays with the line: Enter or Shift+Enter at its
start moves it down with the line, and Backspace that joins the line to the
one before takes it away. *Every so many* is counted by the numbers of the
edition, not by the lines on the page: with numbers every five, the lines
numbered 1235 and 1240 show theirs, whatever lines were passed over between
them.

The numbers in the margin are edited in place: press one, and a field opens
where it stood, with the number the line was given, or empty where it is
counted; Enter gives the line what you wrote, an empty field takes its
number away, and Escape leaves it as it was. Where the pointer rests on a
numbered paragraph, a faint **#** marks the place for a number at each line
that shows none. Lines that are not numbered show nothing to press.

A **screenplay** is written with the kinds of paragraph under *Script*:
**scene heading**, **action**, **character**, **dialogue**,
**parenthetical** and **transition**, each chosen from the kind menu, which
offers every part of a script first while the cursor is in one. The
*Screenplay* layout sets every part where the standard script page has it.

**Two texts side by side**, under *Insert*, puts an original beside its
translation: two columns, each a text of its own, in which verse and
paragraphs are written as anywhere else. Tab goes from the left side to
the right. The document sets them side by side; a word processor gets them
as a table without lines.

**Page break** and **Column break**, under *Insert*, begin what follows on a
new page, or at the top of the next column. Where you write each is a dashed
line across the text with its name on it; every kind of document sets it.
How many columns the text is set in, and the gap between them, is part of
the look of the document: **Columns** and **Gap between columns**, in the
*Page* part of Look (see *The look of a document*). One column is none.

### Kinds of paragraph and of words

Every paragraph is of a **kind**, and a run of words may be: a quotation,
an epigraph, a line of verse, the heading of a scene, a point of a list, a
word in another language. A kind says what the words are, not how they
look, and it is a label and nothing more: no kind follows another, and the
keys are the same in all of them. The look comes from the layout of the
document, which has a look for every kind and changes with the layout;
where you write, the editor shows each kind by cues of its own — a
quotation set in, a speaker in small capitals, a direction in italics —
which go into no document.

You choose one thing: the kind of a paragraph. The button at the left of
the tools shows the kind of the paragraph the cursor is in, or **Mixed**
over a selection of several kinds, and a kind chosen from its menu is given
to the paragraphs selected, or to the one the cursor is in: never more,
never less. Only the kind changes: the lines, the words, the numbering and
what is said of each line stay; an item or a line of verse keeps its
indent, and any other kind has none. Paragraphs of one kind standing
together — lines a verse, quoted paragraphs a quotation, items a list,
lines of code a code block — are set as one where the document has a form
for them; that is the document's business, and nothing you need to tend.

Where you write, paragraphs of one kind standing together are shown by a
thin line at their left, their **bracket**; speakers and directions among
them do not part them, nor have a bracket of their own. Text has none,
unless its lines are numbered. While the cursor is among them, or the
pointer rests on the first, the head of the bracket names their kind:
*Quotation*, *Verse*, *Code*, the name of a kind of your own, and
*· numbered* after it where their lines are numbered. What is said of them
is said there: press the name for its menu. **Numbers…** numbers their
lines (see *Lines and paragraphs, verse, and texts side by side*);
**Language** gives them all a language of its own, as *Language* under
*Words* gives one to what is selected; **Select these paragraphs** selects
them; **Make it text** makes the paragraphs of the kind text, and changes
nothing else, so that a poem keeps its lines; **Begin a new run here** makes
the paragraph the cursor is in begin a bracket of its own, though the one
before is of the same kind, as for two quotations one after the other, which
the document then sets apart too; **Join with the run before** makes them
one again.

The menu shows first the kinds written together with those where the
cursor is — at a line of verse, **Verse**, **Speaker** and **Stage
direction**; in a script, every part; at a headword, **Headword** and
**Gloss** — then the kinds **at hand**: **Text**, **Quotation**, **List**
and **Numbered list**, every kind the project's texts use, and those the
template brings. There is nothing to pin: the menu learns from what you
write. **More…** has every kind, by its group:

| Group | Kinds |
| --- | --- |
| **Text** | **Text** |
| **Quotation** | **Quotation**, words of another, set apart from the text; **Epigraph**, a quotation at the head of a part; **Attribution**, whose words a quotation is, a paragraph of its own after it |
| **Verse** | **Verse**, a stanza of poetry, drama or a transcript, its lines kept as lines; **Speaker** and **Stage direction**: see *Lines and paragraphs, verse, and texts side by side* |
| **Script** | **Scene heading**, **Action**, **Character**, **Dialogue**, **Parenthetical** and **Transition**: the parts of a screenplay |
| **List** | **List**, a point with a mark before it, and **Numbered list**, a point that is counted; an indent makes a list within the list |
| **More** | **Headword** and **Gloss**, the two paragraphs of a glossary; **Code**, a line kept letter for letter; **Break**, a pause between parts, which holds no text and prints the sign the layout gives it; **Draft note**, for your eyes, which goes into no document |

Typing `> `, `- ` or `1. ` at the start of a paragraph of text makes it a
quotation, a point or a numbered point, as Markdown has it; `7. ` begins a
numbered list at 7.

**Words**, among the tools, has the marks beyond italics, bold and small
capitals — **Underlined** (**Ctrl+U**), **Superscript**, **Subscript**,
**Struck through** and **Code in the line** — and then the kinds of words,
which say what the words are: **Foreign words**, with their language,
which the spelling follows and which goes into every document; the
**Title of a work**; a **Term** where it is first used; a **Mention** of a
word spoken of as a word, which stands in the quotation marks of the
language of the text, « » in Norwegian, “ ” in English, „ “ in German; a
**Highlight**, for the eye on the screen; and a **Speaker** or a **Stage
direction** within a line, as an edition sets *Me.* before the words of
Medea or *aside* among them. Select the words first; with nothing selected,
what you type next is of the kind.

**Language**, under **Words**, gives what is selected a language of its
own, from one word to many paragraphs: a Greek quotation, a Latin phrase, a
German paragraph in a Norwegian text; from a bracket it is given to all the
paragraphs beside it at once. It has no look: the words keep their
italics, their kind and all else, and have only a faint dotted line
under them, with the name of their language when the pointer rests on it. The
menu offers the languages of texts; **Other language…** takes any other by
its tag, such as *grc* for Ancient Greek, *hbo* for Biblical Hebrew or *non*
for Old Norse, and offers some that scholars often quote; **The text's own
language** takes it away. Spelling checks the words by the dictionary of
their language, and leaves them alone where there is none, and every document
that is made carries the language, for Word's dictionaries, for the
hyphenation of Typst and LaTeX. Where foreign words say one language and
the words around them another, the latter holds: foreign words are a kind, and
italic, while words in another language are only that.

**Make a kind…**, at the foot of **More…**, makes a kind of paragraph of
your own, and **Make a kind of words…**, under **Words**, a kind of words.
Give it a name, say what it is based on, and say only how it differs:
italic, bold, underlined, letters, alignment, indents, space before and
after, size; what you do not say is as the base has it. A kind of your own
is set as the kind it is based on — one based on a line of verse is a
stanza of verse, and its lines are indented one by one — and paragraphs of
it standing together have a bracket named by it, so that paragraphs of a
kind *Letter* stand together as a letter. What was selected is given the
kind at once. A kind of your own is kept with the project, so that those
you share it with have it, and works in every layout, since it says no more
than how it differs from a kind the layout knows; its name is offered in
your other projects when a kind is named there.

The foot of the kind menu says where the looks come from: **Set as
“Manuscript” has them**, naming the layout of the document, and under it
**How “Quotation” looks…**, named for the kind the cursor is in, which
opens Look beside the preview at the look of that kind, where it is
changed; see *The look of a document*. A draft note and a highlight are for
the screen alone, and go into no document.

### Mathematics

Mathematics is written in the notation of TeX, which is what journals and
publishers take: `x_i` is *x* with a lowered *i*, `\frac{1}{2}` a half. It
is written in a small panel, which shows what is written as it will stand
while you write, and says so when it cannot be read. The symbols that are most
often wanted are in the panel, to be put in by pressing them; what is
selected goes into what is put in.

A **formula** stands in the line, among the words. An **equation** stands on
a line of its own, and can be numbered; what stands around the number,
as in (1), is said by the layout of the document. Pressing either opens it
again.

### Where things stand

A figure, a table and an equation each stand to the left, in the middle or
to the right. The text can flow around a figure or a table that stands at a
side, as it does in books; in the middle, nothing flows around it.

What the layout of the document says holds for all of them: a layout may
have its figures in the middle and its tables at the left margin. Of any one
of them you can say something else, in the panel that opens when it is
pressed; **As the layout** takes what you said back. The panel tells what
the layout says.

Several can stand **beside each other**: open the panel of the second, and
choose **Put it beside the one before it**. A third and a fourth can join
them. Each has its share of the width, its own caption and its own number;
the pictures stand with their feet on one line. **By itself again** takes
one out of the row.

Where you write, things stand as they will in the document. How the text
breaks around a figure differs a little between the kinds of document, as
each has its own way of doing it; the PDF that is set by Typst is what the
preview shows.

### Cross-references to figures, tables, equations and parts

Where the text says *see figure 2*, the number should follow the figure: if
another figure is put in before it, the text is to say *figure 3*. Make such
a cross-reference with **Insert › Cross-reference…**, or **Ctrl+Alt+R**, and
choose what it refers to: a figure, a table, a numbered equation, or a part
of the document, which is an element whose name is printed as a heading.
They can be found by what is said of them.

The cross-reference then says what the document calls the thing:

- a **figure** or a **table** by the word the layout has for it and its
  number, *Figure 2*, *Table 1*, or by the number alone;
- an **equation** by its number as it stands beside it, *(1)*, or by the
  number alone;
- a **part** by its number where the layout numbers the headings, *2.1*, and
  by its name where it does not, or whenever you choose the name.

Words like *see* and *section* you write yourself. Press the cross-reference
to change how it is shown, to go to what it refers to, or to have it refer
to something else.

In a PDF and on a web page a cross-reference leads to what it refers to when
it is pressed. If that is taken away, or left out of the document, it is
shown as **?** in red where you write and as **[?]** in the document, and
the preview remarks on it.

### Reference marks

Some texts are cited by numbers of their own that run through the prose:
the verses of the Bible (*3:16*), the pages of Stephanus in Plato (*327a*),
the numbers of Bekker in Aristotle. Put one in with **Reference mark…**,
under **Numbers** or under **Insert**, or **Ctrl+Alt+K**, and give its
label; the window proposes the one
that follows the mark before it, *3:17* after *3:16* and *327b* after
*327a*. Press a mark to change its label, or to take it away.

A mark is no part of the words: spelling, search and the count of words
pass over it, so that *so loved* is found in *so ¹⁶loved*. Where you write
it is small and raised. How the document sets them all is part of its
look, under *Type* in Look: **small and raised in the line**, or **in
the margin beside their line**, the right one. Word and Writer have no room
in the margin, and raise them; the character style *Reference Mark* can be
changed there.

## Pictures

Pictures are kept in one store for the whole of Glaukopis, as references are
kept in the library. A picture that is used in several texts or projects
is the same picture in all of them, and is kept once.

**Ctrl+3**, or the pictures in the rail at the left, shows the store: every
picture, to be searched by what it is called and by what is said of it. Add
pictures with **Add pictures…**, or drop files on the view.

In a project, **Ctrl+Shift+P** opens the pictures beside the map, where the
references are otherwise. They can be shown for **this map**, which is the
element in view with all under it, for the **project**, or for the whole
**store**. Drag one into a text to make a figure of it there.

Of each picture you can say:

- what it is **called**;
- its **caption**: what figures made with it begin with. What is said of a
  figure can be changed where the figure stands, without changing this;
- what it **shows**, in words, for those who cannot see it;
- **notes**, which are for you, and are part of no document. As with
  references, a note is for all projects when it is written in the store,
  and for the project when it is written in a project, where it is with
  everyone the project is shared with, until you keep it for all projects.

The store also says in which projects a picture is used. A picture that is
removed from the store leaves the figures made with it without a picture.

## Citations

A citation is not text: it is a link to a reference in your library. How it is
printed, as *(Nagy 1979, 73)*, as a footnote, or as a number, is decided by
the reference style of the document, and changes when the style is changed.

So is where it stands beside punctuation. Write the citation where it
belongs in the sentence, before the full stop or after it: a style of notes
sets its mark after the full stop and after a closing quotation mark,
*said.¹*, and a style of author and year sets the citation before the full
stop, *said (Nagy 1979, 73).* A citation with words on both sides stands
where you put it, one whose author you name in your sentence is part of it,
and one you write within brackets stays within them. Nothing in the text
need be moved when the style is changed.

Click a citation to give it more:

- the **page** or another place in the work: chapter, section, verse, line;
- words **before** it, such as *see* or *cf.*;
- words **after** it;
- that the author is named in your sentence, so that only the year is
  given;
- further references, to cite several in one place.

The other way is there too: **Part the words from the citation** takes the
words before and after out into the line, and makes each work a citation of
its own, with its page and nothing else. A note written as one long citation,
*For those who accept Lowe's conclusions see Ussher 1973, xxxv; West 1973,
55; Olson 1996 attributes the view to Lowe…*, becomes text with the
citations in it, to be written on as text.

What you cite is thereby among the references of the project: there is
nothing else to do to put it there. The panel of references
(**Ctrl+Shift+R**) lists them, for this map, which is the element in view
with all under it, for the project, and the whole library. A reference
dragged from the panel is cited where it is dropped in a text; dropped on an
element of the diagram, it is cited at the end of that element's text.

A reference chosen in the panel shows where it is cited in the project,
element by element; a click goes there. In the library, a work
shows the projects that cite it, and a click opens the project at the work.

### What you think of a work

Beside what is cited there is what you make of it: a summary, a doubt, where
it bears on your argument. Such notes belong to the reference, not to the
document. The small notebook that is shown with a reference opens them,
wherever the reference is shown: in the library, in the references of a
project, where a work is chosen to be cited, and in a citation in the text.
Where something is written, the notebook is in view; where nothing is, it
appears when the pointer is near.

There are two kinds:

| | Kept | Seen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **In all projects** | With the reference, in `library.bib` | Wherever you cite the work |
| **In this project** | In the project | In this project, by everyone it is shared with |

What is written in the library is for all projects. What is written in a
project is for that project, until you choose **Keep it for all projects**.

When a project is shared, the notes you have on the works it cites go with
it. To those you share it with they are notes of the project, to read and to
add to; your own stay as you wrote them.

References are found by what is written about them, as by their authors and
titles.

### Loose citations

A text that was written elsewhere has its citations as they were made
there: by Zotero or Mendeley, by a tag such as `[@nagy1979]`, or as words
and nothing else. When such a text is brought in, what could not be made a
citation at once stands in the text as the text it was, with a line of dots
under it. It is a *loose* citation, and waits to be tied to a reference of
your library.

They are gone through in the panel at the side, under its tab with the
magnifying glass, which says how many are left in the element in view and
under it (**Ctrl+Shift+T**). **Loose citations…** in the menu of
the element in view (**This element**, beside its name in the bar) opens it
as well, and says how many there are; and pressing loose text in the text
opens the panel at that one. The panel is there for any element, whether its
text was brought in or written here: what is to be taken for a citation is
said at its top, as below.

The panel shows one at a time, in the sentence it stands in, with the
citation that is proposed for it: for each work the reference, how sure it
is that this is the work and why, the page, and the words before and after.
All of it can be changed as in any citation. Where the library has several
references that it may be, the others are listed; **Another…** looks for
the work in the library. A work that the library does not have, and that
the file tells of, can be **added to the library** from what the file says.
Where the text is open beside the panel, the one that is looked at is shown
there as well: the text is scrolled to it and it is selected,
and a note is opened at it. Where in the list it stands is said over the
passage, with arrows to the one before and the next; **Show the list** opens
the list of all there is.

| To | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Make it a citation | **Make it a citation**, or **Enter** |
| Leave it as the text it is | **Leave it as text** |
| Go on to the next, and decide later | **Later** |
| Go through the list | The arrows up and down, while the panel has the focus |
| Take back what was done | **Ctrl+Z**, one step for each |

A citation that is made takes the place of its text, and reads from then
on as the reference style has it. What is left as text is no longer marked,
and not asked about again. What is put off stays as it was: the marks are
kept in the project, so the citations can be gone through a few today and
the rest another day, and by anyone the project is shared with; the panel
can be closed and opened again at any time.

Where the library has every work of a citation for certain, as by the key
of the item in Zotero, the tag, the DOI or the ISBN, a button at the top
makes citations of **all that are certain** at once. It is undone as one
step. A citation in a note is then treated as when it is gone through by
hand: a note that is nothing but the citation becomes a citation in the
line, and a note that says more keeps it, unless you have said otherwise
for all notes (below). The same holds when citations are made at once as a
document is brought in.

Text that only looks like a citation is not marked. It is looked for while
the panel is open, if you say so at its top, under **What is taken for
citations**:

- **Parentheses with a year in them**, such as *(Nagy 1979, 73)*, and
  with the author in the sentence, *Nagy (1979, 73)*.
- **Notes that name a work of the library**, such as *See Nagy, Best of
  the Achaeans, 73; but cf. Lord, Singer of Tales, 12.*
- **Every note**, those as well in which no work of the library is found,
  for you to find the work.

What is found by how it looks is never certain: a reference is proposed
where the library has one that fits the name and the year, or the name and
the title, and you say whether it is the one. What you leave as text of
these is not proposed again.

Works are told apart most surely by the key of the item in Zotero, which a
reference keeps when the library is brought in from Zotero. References that
came from Zotero before that was so have no key, and are found by their DOI
or ISBN, or by author, year and title. Bringing the library in from Zotero
again gives them their keys; nothing is added twice by it.

Citations made by Zotero are read from Word and OpenDocument files, in the
text, in notes, and where Zotero keeps them in bookmarks; those made by the
Mendeley that writes fields into Word files are read as well. Citations
made by Mendeley Cite, the newer one, are not read, and stay text.

Of a citation in a note you choose what becomes of the note:

- **The note becomes a citation.** The note is taken away, and the citation
  stands where it stood; what the note said beside its works becomes the
  words before and after them. The reference style then sets the citation
  in the line or in a note. This is right for a note that is a citation and
  nothing else. It is not offered where the note holds what a citation
  cannot, such as a formula.
- **The citation stands in the note**, which stays a note with what else it
  says. This is right for a note that says something and cites on the way.

What stood in italics in the words of a note stands upright in a citation
that is made of them.

## The library

The library is one file, `library.bib`, in BibLaTeX. Other tools can read it;
nothing about your references is locked into Glaukopis. Files that belong to
references, such as the PDF of an article, are copied into the library when
they are added. Glaukopis does not depend on where they came from.

**New reference** opens a form with the fields that are usual for the kind of
publication. Other fields are added from **Add field**. The entry as it
stands in the file can be seen and changed with **Edit the source…**, in the
menu of a reference, by those who want to.

References can be added wherever they are used: from the library, from the
picker that **@** opens, and from the panel of references of a project. It
is the same form in all three.

**Collections** gather references for a subject or a piece of work. A
collection holds links, not copies: a reference can be in any number of
collections, and changing it changes it everywhere. Collections can lie
within collections: **New collection inside**, in the menu of a collection,
makes one under it, and the **+** at the head of the collections offers the
same while a collection is in view. A collection is moved under another by
dragging it there, or with **Move to** in its menu. A collection shows its
own references together with those of the collections within it, and counts
them all.

### Finding references

The search field finds references that hold every word typed, in any field,
without regard to case or accents. Beside it, the **filter** button narrows
the list by the **kind** of publication, by the **publisher** and by the
**year**, from one year to another. The filters work together with the
search and with the collection in view; the button says how many are on, and
**Clear the filters** takes them all off.

### A map of the library

**A map of the library…**, in the menu beside *New reference*, makes a tree
of elements of the library: each collection an element, nested as the
collections are, and under it every reference as an element of its own,
named as the list shows it. The text of each is a citation of the reference,
so the bibliography of the tree lists them all, in the reference style
chosen. References in no collection stand at the top. The tree goes **into
the project** you choose, or into **a new project, named after it**. While
a collection is in view, the item is **A map of “…”**, and the tree holds
that collection and what lies within it. The project keeps copies of the
references, as every project does of what it cites.

A project keeps its own copy of every work it cites. A reference deleted from
the library therefore leaves the citations of it as they are; before it is
deleted, Glaukopis says how many projects cite it.

### What is already there

When a reference is added or imported, Glaukopis looks for it in the library.

- It is **the same** if it has the same DOI, or is a whole book with the same
  ISBN, or agrees in everything that tells one work from another.
- It is **probably the same** if title, author and year agree.
- Chapters of one book, editions of one work, and volumes of one set are told
  apart, though they share much.

You are told which, and choose: add it all the same, leave it out, or let the
one that is there take what it lacks from the new one. When several are in
the same case, a row at the head of the import answers for them all at once;
any of them can still be changed on its own. **Find duplicates…**, in the
menu beside *New reference*, goes through the whole library in the same way.

### Looking a reference up

The form for a new reference begins with a field to look it up in. Type or
paste one of these, and press **Enter**:

- a **DOI**, as a number or as an address: the details come from the
  registry the DOI belongs to;
- an **ISBN**: the details come from library catalogues;
- **words** of the title and the name of the author, such as *nagy best
  achaeans*: books are looked for in library catalogues, articles at
  Crossref.

What is found fills in the form, which says where the details are from. They
are yours to correct before the reference is added. Of a book there are often
several records, one for each edition: choose the one you have read.

Catalogues are not faultless. Glaukopis mends what it can, such as titles in
capitals, and says what it has mended.

### PDF files

Drop a PDF on the window, or choose **Add PDF files…** in the menu beside
*New reference*. The file is read for a DOI or an ISBN, which is looked up;
the file is then kept in the library with its reference.

A file that says nothing of what it is, such as a scan, is kept under its
name, for you to fill in the details. A scan can be made searchable: see
*Text in scans and pictures*.

Where the file is dropped decides what becomes of it:

| Dropped on | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| The library | A reference is made of it, in the collection in view |
| A reference that is open | The file is attached to that reference |
| An element of the diagram | A reference is made of it, and cited at the end of the element's text |

Every file of a reference also stands among the files of the library, which
the next section is about.

### The files of the library

The library keeps files as well as references: the PDFs of articles and
books, scans and photographs of pages, papers in Word or OpenDocument,
ebooks, and anything else you want at hand. **Files**, at the head of the
library beside *References*, shows them all (Ctrl+4). A file is copied into
the library when it is taken in, and Glaukopis does not depend on where it
came from.

**Folders.** The files stand in folders of their own, which may lie in
folders, in the tree at the left. **New folder** makes one; a folder is
renamed with F2 or from its menu, and moved by dragging it onto another, or
with **Move to**; **New folder** is in the menu beside **Add files…**. Files are moved by dragging them onto a folder. Deleting a
folder asks what becomes of what is in it: the folders under it **Move up one
level** or are deleted with it (**Delete all folders under this one**), and
the files in the folders deleted are put in the folder it stood in (**Place in
parent folder**) or are deleted from the library, and from their references.
The folders are not the collections: a collection gathers works, and a work
may be in many; a folder is where a file is kept, one place for each.

**Taking files in.** **Add files…** at the head, **Add a folder…** in the
menu beside it, or
files and folders dropped on the view, are taken into the folder in view. A
folder comes with the folders in it, as they were on your disk. A file the
library has already, under any name, is not taken twice: you are told it is
there, and it stays where it was.

**What a file is for.** The pane at the right says what is known of the file
selected: its name, its folder, where it came from and when, its size, and
the references it belongs to. Below are what can be done with a file of its
kind:

| Kind | What can be done |
| --- | --- |
| PDF | Open it; **Make searchable…**; **Import the text as a map…** |
| Picture | Open it; **Read the text…**, to copy or make an element of; **Take into the pictures**, so that it can become a figure |
| Document (Word, OpenDocument, EPUB, HTML, Markdown, Org, plain text) | Open it; **Bring in as elements…** |
| Anything else | Open it with the program your system has for it |

**Files and references.** A file may belong to one reference, to several (a
chapter and its book), or to none yet. **Assign to a reference…**, in the
pane of one file, finds the reference as when you cite. Over several files
there is no such thing: Glaukopis never gives one reference to many files in
one step, since a thousand files given to one book by one click are a
thousand to take back. Files that are parts of one work are added from the
work's side: **Add files of the library…**, in the pane of the reference,
finds them by name, several at once, and its button says how many it adds;
files attached from your disk, by **Attach** or by dropping them on the
pane, are asked about first where there are several. The *×* beside a file
in the pane of a reference takes it from that reference, and **Remove all N
from this reference** takes them all, with one question. **Unassigned
only**, under the filter button, shows the files that belong to none.

**Going through the files.** Where files of the folder in view belong to no
reference, a line at the head of the list says how many, with **Go through
them…**; it stays until they are gone through, or until you hide it, and
comes back when more files are taken in. **Go through these…**, first in
the menu of the files selected and the main button of their pane, does the
same for those. Glaukopis finds out what each file is and proposes a
reference for it: by the DOI or the ISBN printed in it, looked up where your
library does not have it; by its name, where it is named as files often
are, *Nagy 1979 - The Best of the Achaeans*; by the name of its folder,
where the folder is named so, with a year, as a folder of a book's chapters
may be; or by its first page, where a line of it is the title of a work by
an author named there. The reason is said beside every proposal.

The files are listed in four groups: found for certain, found as probable,
new from the services, and nothing found. A row at the head of a group
answers for all of it, and a group of many is folded, to be shown whole on
asking; so a thousand files are a few decisions and a look at the doubtful
ones. For each file you may also take what is proposed, choose another
reference, make a new one, or leave it for later. Nothing is given until you
press the button, which says how many files it gives, and you can stop at
any time: what is left stays unassigned, to be gone through another day.
What a going-through did can be undone at once, by **Undo** in the message
after it, or in the menu beside **Add files…** while Glaukopis is open: the
files are taken back, and the references made in it are removed, their files
staying.

**Files stay.** A file leaves the library only when you remove the file
itself, and you are told first which references it belongs to. Where
removing files leaves folders empty, you are asked whether those folders go
with them; folders that were empty already are not asked about, and stay. Taking a file
from a reference keeps it among the files. When references are removed,
their files stay (**Delete, keep the files**), unless you choose **Delete
with the files**, which removes those that no other reference has.

**Exporting.** **Export…**, in the menu of a folder, of the files selected,
or of a file, and **Export all files…**, write copies into a folder you
choose: the folders become folders on your disk, and every file that belongs
to a reference is named by it, author or editor, year and title, as in
*Snell 1971 - Tragicorum Graecorum Fragmenta.pdf*; a file without a reference
keeps its own name. With **a references.bib**, the works the files belong to
are written beside them, each naming its files where they now are, so that
another reference manager can read the whole.

### Importing

**Import a file…** reads `.bib` files, BibLaTeX or BibTeX. **Paste
references…** takes entries copied from a web page or another program.

**Import from Zotero…** reads the library that Zotero keeps on this
computer: all of it, or one collection, with the files and the notes if you
wish. Zotero is only read; it may be running meanwhile. Its collections
become collections here.

Before anything is added you are shown what was found and what is already
there. Importing the same a second time adds nothing.

## The panel at the side

The look of the document, the references, the pictures, the comments, the
loose citations, the history and the changes to review share one panel at
the side of the map, one at a time. The button with the panel on it, in the bar over the
map, opens it as it was last, and closes it; the tabs at its head change
what it shows. Each has its key as well: **Ctrl+Shift+A**, **Ctrl+Shift+R**, **Ctrl+Shift+P**,
**Ctrl+Shift+M**, **Ctrl+Shift+T**, **Ctrl+Shift+H** and **Ctrl+Shift+E**.
What the panel shows is of the element in view, with all under it, named in
the line over its tabs: with two views side by side, that of the pane in
view, whose head is marked the same way, and the line is marked with it. The
history alone is of the whole project, and has no such line.

## Kinds of elements

An element can be of a **kind**: a character, a place, an event, a source,
an argument, a scene — whatever the work needs, named by you. A kind is a
name and a colour, and may have a text that an element of the kind begins
with: *Appearance, Wants, Fears*, one paragraph each. It may also say,
under **An element of this kind writes in**, in which kind of paragraph
the text of its elements begins: the text of a scene with a scene heading,
that of an entry of a glossary with a headword. A paragraph that is given
another kind keeps it.

A kind may also say what an element of it is in the document, under **In
the document** in the kind's dialog: an abstract, an appendix, a page of its
own, chosen among the kinds that come with Glaukopis (see *What goes into
the document*). An *Author's note* that is a front section is then printed
before the text, in every layout. A kind that is **nothing in particular**
there is a name and a colour, as before.

**Kind** in the element's menu has the kinds of the project, in their
colours; then the kinds that come with Glaukopis which the template puts in
hand, or which the project uses already, such as **Abstract** or
**Appendix**; and **More…**, with all of them by group: **Front matter**,
**Back matter** and **Cover**. **New kind…** makes a kind and gives it to
the element, and **Kinds of this project…** shows the project's own, with
how many elements are of each, to change or delete them. A name you have
used in another project is offered as you type, with the colour it had
there.

An element of a kind carries the colour at its left edge in the diagram,
as a dot in the outline, and as a small label over its name in the text; an
element of a kind that comes with Glaukopis carries the kind's name in
plain grey. Nothing of it goes into the document, unless the kind says what
the element is there.

## Timelines

An element can say **when it is**: at a point, or over a span. Choose **Say
when it is…** in its menu. Each end of the placement is either a time
written in words — `431 BC`, `c. 480 BCE`, `May 1453`, `1453-05-29`, `5th
century BC`, `the 1920s` — or relative: *after* another element, *before*
one, *between* two, or *during* one. A year stands for the whole year, a
century for the whole century: what is not known more finely is not drawn
more finely. **Approximately** marks an end as a guess. A written time may
also be given a margin either side, **Give or take** `5 years`, `3 months`,
`10 days`, or a number of the timeline's units: the time is drawn where it
was written, with a band that fades away on both sides as far as the
margin reaches, and what is placed relative to it is allowed that room.

Once anything under the element in view says when it is, it can be seen as
a **Timeline**, beside *Diagram* and *Text*. Each child of the element in
view is a lane; the placed elements of its branch stand in it, and its own
placement, if it has one, is the span of the lane — a person's lifetime, a
city's years. A lane in which nothing says when it is, its own element
included, is not shown: what says nothing of its time stays out of the
timeline. The button with the rows among the timeline's controls chooses the
lanes: any branch can be one lane, or each of its children a lane of their
own. An element that stands for another element brings that element's
placed elements with it. Elements that are in no lane stand in a lane of
their own at the foot. Where you have chosen the lanes, a new child of the
element in view is not a lane until you choose it, and stands at the foot
too: the lanes button is marked while lanes are chosen, and pressing the
name of the lane at the foot opens the choice. An element that is deleted
leaves the choice; when none that was chosen is left, each child of the
element in view is a lane again. The lanes are of the element in view: enter
another element, and its children are the lanes.

A written time stands where it was written. What is relative is **solved**:
the timeline works out the window of what it may be, from everything else,
and draws it dashed in the middle of that window, with the window as a
faint band — the uncertainty is seen, not hidden. An element that is after
something and before something else that comes earlier **cannot be placed**,
and is marked; one that refers to an element that says nothing of its time
is listed at the foot. Where nothing in view has a written time, the
timeline is **in order** only, each element a step after what it comes
after, with no scale.

A kind of element can be lanes too: under the lanes, **Lanes by kind**
makes every element of a kind — each character, each place — a lane of
its own. **Add a chronology to the map**, among the timeline's controls,
adds an element under the element in view with a table of everything
placed, in the order of time, with when and what: a copy of what the
timeline knows at the moment, to be written on and printed with the
document.

The timeline has an **axis**: the dates of the world, or **units of your
own** for an invented one — years, days, cycles, whatever you call them — in
which times are numbers: `Year 12`, `Day 3`, or just `12`. Choose the axis
under the lanes: it is set on the element in view, and holds for everything
under it that sets none.

Scroll with Shift or drag the timeline to move along it; Ctrl with the
wheel, or the buttons, change the scale. A click chooses an element; a
double click opens **When it is** for it; the right button gives its menu,
in which **Write text** opens it in the text.

Nothing moves by mistake: the arrows button among the controls, **Move by
dragging**, must be on before an element can be dragged along the axis,
the edges of a span dragged to change its start or its end, and the outer
edge of a margin dragged to make it wider or narrower. While you drag, the
time reads beside the pointer as it changes. A time that is dragged is
written anew as finely as it was written: a year stays a year, a month a
month, and `431 BC` stays written with BC; a margin keeps its words. What
is placed relative to another element is not dragged; it stands where it
was solved.

Press the name of a lane to fold its elements away and press it again to
open them; its own span stays. A double click on the name opens **When it
is** for the lane's element.

An element that says nothing of its time is not on the timeline, and a
child's time need not lie within its parent's. When **Elements without a
time** is on, each such element is shown as a card, as in the diagram, in a
box at the foot of the area of the nearest placed element over it: under
that element's row, from its left edge, an element deeper down with its path
before its name; what has no placed element over it in its lane stands in
a box at the foot of the lane. The box says where the element is in the
map, not when it is: nothing is written on it, the chronology does not list
it, and it is not counted among what could not be placed. Press a card to
say when it is in words, or drag it along the lane to place it at that time.

The elements that say nothing of their time, with nothing over them that
does, are not on the timeline, but they are near: **Elements without a
time**, beside the arrows, shows each in the lane of its branch, the
elements deeper down with their path, and the branches in which nothing is
placed yet as lanes of their own. Drag one along its lane to place it at
that time, as finely as the axis is looked at, or press it to say when it is
in words.

## Comments

A comment is a note on an element, or on a passage of its text: a question
to yourself, a doubt, a word to someone you write with. It is never part of
the text, and goes into no document.

- **On a passage:** select the words and press **Comment** in the bar over
  the text, or **Ctrl+Alt+C**. The panel opens with the passage quoted, and
  you write under it; Enter sends it.
- **On an element:** **Comment** with nothing selected, or **Comment…** in
  the element's menu, in the diagram or from the grip beside a heading.

A commented passage is tinted when the pointer rests on it, or on its
thread in the panel; the name of an element with open comments carries a
line at its left, and a small mark in the right margin counts the open
comments on the element; press the passage or the mark to see the thread.
In the diagram an element with open comments stands out a little, more
while the pointer rests on one of its threads in the panel, and carries
the same mark with its count; press it, and the comments hang under the
element as **cards**, like children that are not in the tree; press it
again to hide them. The button with the speech bubble among the diagram's
controls shows every open comment so, at once. A card can be dragged where
it is wanted; pressing it opens the thread.

In the panel the threads of one element stand together under its name;
press the name to go to the element, twice to write in it.

A comment is a **thread**: the first note, and answers under it, each with
who wrote it and when. You change or delete your own notes; you answer
anyone's. A thread that is dealt with is **settled** from the tick at its
head, which takes its marks away; the panel shows the open threads, the
settled ones, or all, and a settled thread can be opened again.

When an element is deleted, its comments go with it. When the words a
comment was on are deleted, the comment stays on the element, with the
words it was on quoted. Comments travel with a shared project, and are kept
in its history like everything else.

## One tree, entered anywhere

A project is one tree, and any element of it can be **entered**: it is then
the element in view, at the centre of the diagram, the title of the text and
the document of the preview, with all that is under it, and the rest of the
project is out of the way. The project itself is an element, at the top: a
new project opens there, with everything in it. Enter an element with the
small round button that appears at its corner in the diagram, when the
pointer is on it or it is selected, and before its heading in the text, when
the pointer is on the heading; with **Alt+Shift+Down**; or with
**Open as a map** in its menu, in the diagram and from the grip beside its
heading in the text. Go up with **Alt+Shift+Up**; with the button of the same kind
on the element in view, at its corner in the diagram and before the title in
the text; or by pressing the name of the element over it, which stands small
at the left of the bar over the map. The project itself has no such button:
the owl at the left of the bar goes up from it, to the map of the projects,
at the folder the project is in.

The bar says where you are: the name of the element over the one in view,
small, which goes up; then the name of the element in view, which pulls down
the elements beside it, to show one of them in its place, or, for the
project itself, the trees under it. Beside the name is **This element**,
with what can be done with the element in view: **Rename**, **Document
settings…**, **Loose citations…**, **Languages…**, which opens
the document settings at the language, **New element under it**, **A
document into it…** and **Delete**, which takes the element
with all under it, and goes up. The project itself is not deleted here, but
from the home page. Over that bar, the bar of the project has **Diagram**,
**Text**, **Side by side**, **Preview** and **Share**; side by side, each
side has a bar of its own.

**The list of projects** (**Ctrl+Shift+L**) shows the whole tree of the open
project under its name, folded but for the way down to the element in view,
which is marked; press an element to enter it, and the chevron before one
to show or hide what is under it.

This is what makes it safe to write. A tree that is valuable as it is, the
first sketch of the argument, or the plan for the whole book, need not be
consumed by the writing:

- A thought that has no place yet is a tree of its own under the project:
  **New element here**, in the menu of the diagram's background, or a double
  click there, makes one under the element in view where you clicked. Move
  it into the argument when it has found its place.
- **Copy** a branch with **Ctrl+C**, paste it beside itself with
  **Ctrl+V**, and write in the copy. A copy remembers where it came from,
  and what its original was. When the original changes, the copy is marked;
  the mark, or **Compare with the original…** in its menu, shows where the
  two differ now. The copy can then take the original's name and text, or
  keep its own.
- **Copy to** and **Move to**, in the menu of an element, list the project
  itself and the other trees under it: a chapter is lifted out of the plan
  for the book as a tree of its own, or a tree is put into the book where it
  belongs. Enter the tree to work in it alone. **Choose where…**, first in
  both, shows the whole tree, with a field that finds an element by its
  name, so that the elements can be put under any element of the project;
  what cannot take them, they themselves and what is under them, is grey.
- **To another project** as well: below them, **Copy to** and **Move to**
  list the other projects; the elements go in under the top of the project
  chosen, without its having to be open, and **Show** in the message that
  follows opens it. Or copy them with **Ctrl+C** in the diagram, open the
  other project, and paste them with **Ctrl+V**. Cut them with **Ctrl+X**
  instead, and they move: they stay where they are until they are pasted,
  and within the project the move is one step, which one **Ctrl+Z** takes
  back. **Cut**, **Copy** and **Paste under it** are in the menu of an
  element, in the diagram and in the text, and **Paste here** in the menu of
  the diagram's background, which puts what is pasted under the element in
  view, where you clicked. They go with their names, texts and notes, their
  kinds, colours and times, and all that is under them; kinds the project
  made for itself go with them, unless the other project has kinds of the
  same names. Comments stay behind, and a copy in another project does not
  remember its original. A move to another project can be undone here,
  which brings the elements back, but leaves the copy there.
- An element can have **another element take its place in the document**:
  **Stand for another element**, in its menu, lists the trees under the
  project, and **Choose an element…** any element of it; what would make the
  document hold itself cannot be chosen. The tree of the book then has one element for each chapter, each
  of which stands for the tree of that chapter, written elsewhere in the
  project. The preview of the book shows the book.
- **Side by side**, in the bar of the project, shows the project as diagram
  and as text beside one another; enter another element on either side to
  see two parts of the project, and move elements between them, by dragging
  or by cutting and pasting. Each side can be diagram, text or timeline.
- **Another project beside**: **Open beside**, in the list of projects at
  the left, shows another project in the second side. Then both bars begin
  with the name of their project, with its sign, and the line between them is
  doubled, so that the two are not taken for one. Elements dragged from one
  to the other are copied there, or moved with a plain drop, as **Copy to**
  and **Move to** another project do; undo takes a move back in both. The
  keys, and undo, work in the side you are in. The panel at the side, the
  preview and the history stay with your own project; the other is shown as
  diagram or text, and closes with its side.

The line between the two sides can be dragged, as can those beside the
preview, the references and the outline. A double click on a line puts it
back.

A project made before this version, with maps, opens as one tree: each map
is a tree under the project, folded, with the map's settings on it, and the
elements that were loose stand after them.

### A document you have written

A text that was written elsewhere can be brought in, and becomes a tree of
elements under the element in view. The file it came from is read and not
changed.

- In a project, **A document into it…** in the menu of the element in view
  (**This element**, in the bar over the map), or drop the file on the bar,
  or on the window.
- Among the projects, **A project from a document…**, or drop the file
  there. The project is named after the document.

The title becomes an element under the element in view, the top of what is
brought in. Every heading becomes an element under the heading above it,
with the text under the heading as its text; what stands before the first
heading becomes the text of the element at the top. Author, date, keywords
and language are set on that element, as the details of its document; the
abstract becomes an element of the kind **Abstract**, first under the top
(see *What goes into the document*).

Before anything is made you are shown what was found: the title, which you
can change, how many parts, words, notes, figures, tables and citations, and
under **To know** what could not be brought in as it was. **Cancel** leaves
everything as it was; **Undo** takes the elements away again as one step.

| Kind of file | Endings |
| --- | --- |
| Word | `.docx` |
| OpenDocument | `.odt` |
| Markdown | `.md`, `.markdown` |
| HTML | `.html`, `.htm` |
| LaTeX | `.tex` |
| Rich Text | `.rtf` |
| EPUB | `.epub` |
| Org, reStructuredText, Typst | `.org`, `.rst`, `.typ` |
| Plain text | `.txt` |

AsciiDoc, DocBook, JATS, FictionBook, OPML, MediaWiki, Textile, Djot, Muse
and Jupyter notebooks can be chosen in the dialog for files as well. All but
plain text are read by Pandoc. Of plain text every line break is kept: a
lone one is a break within the paragraph, an empty line parts paragraphs,
and each further empty line is an empty paragraph; a file without any empty
line is read as a paragraph to a line. In Markdown, Org and reStructuredText
too a line break is a break, and an empty line parts paragraphs; several
empty lines count as one.

What becomes of what is in the document:

- **Notes** become notes. A note on a heading stands at the beginning of the
  text under it.
- **Pictures** are taken into the store of pictures, and stand in the text as
  figures, with what is said of them. Those that a Word or OpenDocument file
  holds are called after the document. A picture that is to be fetched from
  the network is left out: nothing is fetched.
- **Tables** become tables, with their headings, cells that span, and what
  is said of them.
- **Captions** are found where the document has them: with the figure or
  the table, or as a paragraph beside it, as Word and LibreOffice write
  them, also where the picture stands in a frame. *Figure 1:* before the
  words is left out, since the document numbers its figures and tables
  itself.
  Where the running text says *see Figure 1*, that stays text as it was
  written; put a cross-reference in its place if the number is to follow.
- **Mathematics** is kept as it is written, in the line or as equations.
- **Citations** written by key (Markdown `[@homer]`, LaTeX `\cite{homer}`)
  become citations when the key is in your library, with page and words
  before and after. Those that are not, and citations made by Zotero or
  Mendeley in Word and OpenDocument files, stay the text they were written
  as, and are marked as *loose* citations. You are told how many
  there are, and choose whether those that Zotero made of works your
  library has are made citations at once, and whether the rest are gone
  through when the document is brought in: see *Loose citations*.
- **A list of works cited** is brought in as text. The document makes its
  own from what is cited in it, so the part can be left out of the document
  or deleted.
- **Code, lists of terms, lines across the page** and what is written for
  one kind of document only have no place in the tree: what has text is kept
  as paragraphs, the rest is left out, and you are told.

A file with changes that are tracked is brought in as it stands when all of
them are accepted; comments in the margin of a Word file are left out. You
are told of both. The language of the text is not taken from Word and
OpenDocument files, which say what language the computer had, and not
always that of the text; a web page, Markdown or LaTeX that says a passage
is in another language keeps it as the language of the passage. OpenDocument files lose their mathematics in the
reading, which is a limit of Pandoc.

A PDF or a picture can be brought in the same way, and has its text read:
see *Text in scans and pictures*.

## Text in scans and pictures

A scan is a picture of a page: its text cannot be searched, copied or
quoted until it is read. Glaukopis reads it with Tesseract, a program that
reads text in pictures, in four places.

- **A PDF or a picture becomes a tree of elements.** In a project, choose
  **A document into it…** in the menu of the element in view, and choose
  the file, or drop the file on the bar over the map; dropped elsewhere in a
  project, a PDF is taken into the library and a picture becomes a figure.
  Among the projects, **A project from a document…**, or drop the file
  there. An element is made for the PDF under the element in view, named by
  the title found on its pages, else as the PDF names itself, else after its
  file. With the shape of the pages kept (below), the document becomes a map
  **by its headings**: the headings of the text are elements under it, each
  level under the one above, holding what follows each; what stands before
  the first heading, an author or a date, is the document element's own
  text; and a mark with the page's number as it is printed (*xiv*, *23*)
  stands where each page began, so that what you quote can still be found on
  its page, unless **Mark where each page begins** is turned off. **By its
  pages** makes an element of each page instead, named by its number, with
  the headings under it, as an edition cited by its pages may want; it is
  what is made where no heading is found. Before anything is made, the
  window shows the title and the headings as they will nest. The text of a
  picture becomes the text of the one element made of it.
- **A PDF of the library is made searchable.** In the files of a reference,
  or in the pane of a file among the files of the library, **Make
  searchable…** reads its pages and lays
  their text unseen under what is shown. The PDF looks as it did, and its
  text can be searched, selected and copied in any program that shows PDFs.
  The file in the library is replaced by the searchable one, which the
  reference then points to, as does every other reference that had the same
  file.
- **A PDF of the library becomes elements.** Beside it, or in the pane of
  the file, **Import its text as a map…** reads the PDF where it is stored, and makes elements of what
  is read, as of a PDF brought in, **into the project** you choose, under
  the element in view where that project is open, or into **a new project,
  named after it**. The file stays in the library as it was.
- **The text of a picture of the store is read.** **Read the text in it…**,
  in the pane of a picture, shows the text, to be copied. Where the picture
  is looked at in a project, beside the map, **Make an element of it** makes
  an element of the text there, under the element in view.

Many PDFs have text already: those made from a written document, and scans
that a scanner or another program has read. Where a PDF has text, the window
grows wide: beside the choices stands **the whole text the PDF has**, page by
page under the numbers the pages have in print, to be scrolled through before
anything is decided. A line above the choices says what was found: on how
many pages there is text, in which fonts, and how the text keeps its letters
where Glaukopis can tell.

Then one choice, in the same words wherever a PDF is read, into elements or
to be made searchable:

- **Keep the text the PDF has.** Its pages with text are taken as they are,
  and only the pages without text are read.
- **Translate it into readable text.** Offered where the text keeps its
  letters in a way Glaukopis knows (below). The text is made readable by
  what the fonts say of their letters; no page is read from a picture for
  it, and the preview shows the text as it will be. In a searchable PDF,
  the PDF's own tables are put right instead (**Make readable**), so that
  every reader copies and searches the text rightly; nothing that is shown
  changes.
- **Read every page anew with Tesseract.** Only what Tesseract reads from
  pictures of the pages is kept, and the text the PDF has is taken from no
  page. In a searchable PDF its unseen text is taken away, and what is read
  is laid in its place; the pages look as they did.

What is chosen at first follows what was found: translating where the text
can be translated, reading anew where it does not read as text and cannot
be, and keeping otherwise. A page that Tesseract cannot read is never given
the PDF's text in its place: it says, in a line of its own, that it could
not be read, and the window says how many pages were read, taken and not
read.

**How a text may keep its letters.** Many scholarly editions set their
Greek in fonts that keep the letters under other names, so that a PDF
reader copies Latin signs or nothing where Greek stands. Glaukopis knows
three such ways, and says which it found and in which fonts:

- *Greek glyph names*, as the Porson fonts of Oxford and Teubner texts name
  their letters (*etaasper*, *alphatildeiota*): each becomes the letter with
  its breathing, accent and iota below, and an accent or breathing drawn
  apart from its letter is put onto it. The sigmas come out as σ and ς by
  their place in the word, whatever shape the print gives them.
- *Small capitals and old-style figures* kept by name, as many book fonts
  keep them: they become their letters and digits.
- *Beta Code*, Greek written in Latin letters and signs (*mh=nin a)/eide*).

A searchable PDF made readable keeps two limits: its tables give one letter
for each shape, so a sigma printed in one shape for all places is copied as
ς everywhere; and an accent or breathing drawn apart from its letter is
copied as a mark after the letter, which most programs show on it. The text
read into elements has every sigma right, and every letter with its accents
as the one character Unicode has for it (ἄ, not α with two marks after it),
whether it was kept, translated or read by Tesseract.

**Scripts whose letters look alike.** Where the languages chosen are of two
scripts with letters of the same shapes, Greek and Latin, Cyrillic and
Latin, or Greek and Cyrillic, the window says so, and names the letters:
Tesseract then often reads a letter as the other script's, the lunate sigma
as a Latin *c*, a Cyrillic *р* as a Latin *p*, and whole words come out
wrong. For one script alone, take the others away. Rows of accents over
closely set Greek, which Tesseract may read as lines of their own, are left
out of what it reads.

Before anything is read, you are asked for the languages of the text, the
likeliest first. Those chosen under *Settings* are given at first; where
none are chosen there, the language of the text, as far as it is known (that
of new texts, of the project, or of the reference), and that of the
interface. Tesseract reads only the
languages it has data for, and each one more makes the reading slower, and
not always better. The data of more languages is imported under *Settings* ›
*Language* › *OCR*, by **Import OCR language files**, as the dictionaries of
spelling are (see *Spelling*): from the server of Glaukopis, which offers
the best data there is for English, Norwegian, Swedish, Danish, German,
French, Italian, Ancient Greek and Latin, for now. The list of **OCR
languages** says of each whether it is the system's or imported, and **×**
takes an imported one away. A language you have imported is read with
before the computer's of the same name. **Default OCR settings**, below,
are how a text is read at first. A page takes a few seconds; several are read at a time,
and you are shown how many are read. **Cancel** stops the reading, and
nothing is made or changed.

What is read is set in paragraphs. A word broken with a hyphen at the end of
a line is joined again when the next line begins with a small letter and the
part before the hyphen does not look like a name: *for-* and *skning* make
*forskning*, but *Oslo-* and *avtalen* make *Oslo-avtalen*. A number that
stands alone at the head or the foot of a page, which is its number, is left
out, and so are lines without a letter or a digit, which are what is read
of a rule or a picture on the page.

**The shape of the pages.** Where **Keep the shape of the pages** is chosen,
as it is at first, the shape of each page is read from where its lines stand
and how large they are, the same for the text a PDF has and for what
Tesseract reads, and kept in the elements made:

- *Headings*, a line or two set larger or bolder than the text and standing
  apart from it, become elements, each holding what follows it up to the
  next heading. Their levels, three at most, come from the surest source
  there is: the outline a PDF made from a written document carries of its
  own headings, which also finds those set no larger than the text; their
  numbers, *1*, *1.2*, *1.2.3*, where two or more are numbered; else their
  sizes over the whole file. The first heading, where it is larger than every
  other and alone in its size, is the title, and names the document.
- *The text goes on across the pages*: a paragraph that runs over a page's
  edge is one paragraph, with the smaller text that stood at the page's foot
  after it, and lines kept as lines go on as one block.
- *Lines kept as lines*, set from one left edge and ragged at the right, as
  verse, addresses, lists and transcripts are, stay lines: one paragraph of
  text with a break at the end of each line. What they are is yours to say,
  by giving the paragraph a kind.
- *Numbers in the margin* beside lines, or at the end of lines in one column
  with them, become the numbers given to those lines, on the side they stood,
  and the paragraph is numbered by **Only the numbers given** (see *Lines
  and paragraphs, verse, and texts side by side*). They are taken so only
  where they count upward down the page, so that the figures of a table, a
  year in a sentence or a number a note cites stay in the text.
- *Paragraphs* are joined from their lines as above. *Italic*, **bold** and
  small capitals are kept where the fonts of the PDF say so; Tesseract tells
  no fonts, and what it reads has none.
- *Running heads and page numbers*, a line at the head or the foot that
  recurs on three pages or more and a number at an edge that steps from page
  to page, are left out: the page's element bears its number already.
- *Two columns* are read the left before the right. Tables are not read:
  their lines are kept as lines.

Without it, the pages become plain paragraphs, as above.

**Styles found.** Text that recurs over several pages in one look other than
that of the body and the headings, such as smaller text at the foot of the
pages, or indented text set apart, is a style found. After the reading,
under **Styles found**, each is shown with a sample in its look and how often
it occurs, and you say under **What it is** what it is: a kind of paragraph
of the project, **A kind of your own…**, named there and made when the
document is brought in, with no look until you give it one, or text, which
it is where you say nothing. One name given to two styles makes one kind.

Glaukopis does not guess what a text is. It does not call lines verse,
smaller text notes or an apparatus, or a page a letter or a scene: it finds
the shape a page has, names what it found by its look, and leaves to you
what it means.

Reading is not faultless: letters that are faint, worn, or in a type or a
script the data of the language does not know are misread. Check what is
read against the page where it matters.

**When a reading goes badly**, three things can be tried, under **How it is
read** in the dialog, for that reading, or under Tesseract in *Settings*, for
every reading: a higher **resolution** (400 or 600 dots to the inch in place
of 300) where the print is small; the **layout** told, one column where
columns are muddled together, one block of text for a single paragraph, or
sparse text where words stand apart on the page; and **black and white**
where the print is faint or uneven, so that what is pale is read as ink.

A PDF that is locked with a password cannot be made searchable, since what
is added would have to be locked in the same way. Its text can still be
brought into a project as elements, where its pages can be drawn.

## Searching

**Ctrl+F** searches the text in view, where you write, and **Ctrl+H**
searches and replaces. A bar opens over the text. Type the words: what is
found is marked where it stands, the one that is shown more strongly, and
the bar says which it is of how many: *3 of 17*. **Enter** or **F3** shows
the next, **Shift+Enter** or **Shift+F3** the one before. **Escape** closes
the bar and leaves the cursor at what was found, selected.

All of the text is searched, not only what is in view: the names of the
elements, their text, the notes, what is said of figures and tables, and
their cells, in elements that are folded away and in those left out of the
document as well. What is shown is opened: what it is folded away under,
and the note it is in.

Beside the words are the options:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Aa** | Capitals as they are written; otherwise *wrath* finds *Wrath* |
| **ab** | Whole words only; otherwise *wrath* finds *wrathful* |
| **é=e** | Letters with and without accents alike: *αειδε* finds *ἄειδε*, *Pelee* finds *Pelée* |
| **.\*** | A regular expression |
| The dashed frame | Only in the text you selected before you searched |
| The label | Citations as they are shown, formulas and cross-references as well |

Words are found as they are written, but room of any kind is room, as a
space that does not break, and an apostrophe or a quotation mark may be
straight or curly.

To replace, write what replaces in the second field. **Replace**, or
**Enter** in that field, replaces the one that is shown and shows the next;
**Replace all** replaces all that is found. What is put in has the marks of
what it replaces: a word in italics stays in italics. Only text is replaced:
citations, formulas and cross-references are not, and the option to search
them is not there while you replace. All that one **Replace all** did is
taken back at once, by **Undo** or by **Ctrl+Z** in the text. With a
regular expression, `$1` in what replaces stands for what its first group
found, `$2` for the second, and so on.

In a regular expression, `\n` is a break within a paragraph and `\p` a
paragraph break, both in what is searched for and in what replaces it.
`ends\.\pIt` finds a paragraph that ends with *ends.* and the one after it
that begins with *It*, and replacing it with `ends. It` makes the two one
paragraph; replacing `; ` with `;\p` begins a new paragraph after every
semicolon, and `\n` in what replaces breaks the line there. A search with
`\p` reads across the paragraphs of the text of one element, not into its
name, its notes, the captions of figures and tables, the cells of a table, or
from one side of a parallel text to the other. (`\p{L}` and the other
classes of Unicode keep their meaning.)

The edit box of an element in the diagram can be searched in the same way,
its name and its text. In the diagram itself, **Ctrl+F** turns to the text
and searches it.

### Searching through everything

**Ctrl+Shift+F**, or the magnifying glass at the left edge of the window,
searches all you have written: the project you had open last, or all
projects. What is found is listed by project, and within it by the tree
under the project, each with the words around it; choose one, and the
project opens there, with the words in the bar over the text.

With the last of the options, what stands outside the texts is searched as
well: citations as they are shown, formulas and cross-references, the
details of the documents, what you have written about the works cited in
each project, and the words that name associations.

A project that is not open is read for the search, which takes a moment for
a large one. It is read again only when it has changed.

## What goes into the document

Everything under the element in view, in its order: the element's name is
the title, its text comes first, and each element under it is a part, with
the parts under that, as deep as they go. Any element prints so: the project
itself prints the whole project, and a chapter that is entered prints the
chapter. Two things change that, both in the menu of an element:

- **Print the name as a heading.** When this is off, the name is for you: a
  label for a paragraph, which is printed without it.
- **Leave out of the document.** The element stays in the tree, with what is
  under it, and is not printed, in no document that holds it. A thought not
  yet decided on is kept so, out of every document; or the book is one tree
  under the project, with the thoughts as trees beside it, and that tree is
  what is printed.

What a document is set by, its template, layout, reference style, language,
authors, date, keywords and identifiers, is set on an element and holds for
everything under it that sets none of its own: set once on the project, it
holds for every chapter printed alone. The title and the subtitle are an
element's own, and never taken from above: a chapter printed alone is not
titled as the book. See *Templates* and *The document settings*.

An element can also be a **part** of the document: the abstract, the
dedication, an appendix, the acknowledgements, the cover. Such an element is
of one of the kinds that come with Glaukopis (see *Kinds of elements*), and
is written in the tree as any other; the kind says where it is printed. The
**front matter** is printed before the text and the body, the **back
matter** after the last part of the body, each where its own place says, and
the **cover** on no page of the document: an element of the kind **Cover** is
what the e-book takes its cover from, and a cover of its own is made by a
later version. A part belongs to the element it stands directly under: the
abstract under the book is the book's, and the abstract under chapter three
is chapter three's, printed at the head of the chapter when the book is
printed and as the abstract of the document when the chapter is printed
alone. Of a kind a document has one of, such as the abstract, the first
among the elements beside one another is used; the others are marked on
their label, and the checklist of the document settings names them. Nothing
is lost: a part the template has no place for is printed all the same, after
the parts the template lists in front or at the back.

## Templates

A **template** says what a document consists of: its parts in their order,
the kinds of elements and of paragraph it puts in your hand, the layout and
the reference style it brings, and what it requires, such as how many words
it may have. A journal article has a title page, an abstract and keywords,
the text, then appendices, acknowledgements, notes and bibliography; a novel
has a cover, a half-title and a title page, a copyright page, a dedication
and an epigraph, the contents, the chapters, then the acknowledgements and a
note on the author. Three things are told apart, each with one job:

| | What it says |
| --- | --- |
| **Template** | What the document consists of |
| **Layout** | How the page looks: the page, the type, the spacing, the headings, the notes, the looks of the kinds |
| **Reference style** | How citations and the bibliography are written |

A template is set on an element and holds for everything under it, as the
layout and the reference style do: the template of the book holds for a
chapter printed alone. Any element may have one of its own, so a thin
element that stands for a chapter (see *One tree, entered anywhere*) sends
the chapter as an article, with the journal's template, while the book keeps
its own. Where an element chooses neither a layout nor a style, it has those
the template brings; where the template brings none, it has your usual
ones. Your usual template, layout and reference style are chosen under
*Settings*, and hold wherever nothing else is said.

The templates that come with Glaukopis are offered by group, with a search:

- **Plain**: the **plain document**, nothing but the title, the text, the
  notes and the bibliography.
- **Articles and papers**: a **paper**, a student's or a conference paper,
  as the plain document with your usual layout and reference style; and a
  **journal article**, with an abstract and keywords before the text, and
  appendices and acknowledgements after it, with no limits until a journal's
  template sets them.
- **Books and theses**: an **academic book**, with half-title and title
  page, copyright page, dedication and epigraph, the contents, foreword,
  preface and acknowledgements, the chapters, then appendices, glossary,
  notes, bibliography and a note on the author; a **thesis**, with its
  abstract and acknowledgements, the contents with the lists of figures and
  tables, the chapters, then appendices and bibliography; and a **report**,
  with abstract, contents, text, appendices and bibliography.
- **Fiction**: a **novel**, as a printed book or an e-book, with its cover,
  its front matter and its chapters, in the layout of a novel as a book; a
  **novel manuscript**, a title page and the chapters in the standard
  manuscript layout, for an agent or a publisher; a **short story**, for a
  magazine or a competition, the same way; and a **collection of stories**,
  as a book, each story an element.
- **Stage and screen**: a **play**, with its cast and its setting before the
  acts and scenes, the speeches written as verse with their speakers and
  directions; and a **screenplay**, the script on the standard page.
- **Poetry**: **poems**, a collection with a dedication, the contents, each
  poem an element, then acknowledgements and a note on the poet.
- **Other kinds of text**: a **news article**, with a standfirst over the
  text; a **blog post**, the text alone, as a web page or as Markdown for a
  site that makes its own pages; and a **letter**, the text and a signature.
- **Journals**, **publishers** and **style guides**: one for each journal,
  publisher and style guide that Glaukopis has a layout for, based on the
  article, the book or the paper, each keeping the layout and the reference
  style it had before, and its limits as requirements. *The
  Classical Quarterly* allows twelve thousand words, footnotes and abstract
  included, an abstract of 250 words and eight keywords, and asks for an
  anonymised text. Each says where its requirements were found, and when,
  and how sure that is.

**Change this template…**, under the template chosen in the document
settings, opens the template editor on it, and **New template…** opens it
on a new one. The editor has, part by part:

- **About this template**: its description, its group, what it is based on,
  and where its requirements are from.
- **Layout and reference style**: what it brings, or your usual.
- **Contents**: the order of the document, as a list. In it stand the parts
  Glaukopis makes itself, the half title, the title page, the keywords, the
  table of contents, the lists of figures and tables, the text, the notes and
  the bibliography, and *Every abstract*, *Every appendix*, and so on, which
  place every element of that kind, in the order of the tree. Each is moved
  up and down or taken out, and **A part…** and **A kind of element…** add
  one. A kind listed before the text is front matter, one listed after it
  back matter.
- **Kinds of elements**: the kinds the template puts in the hand, so that
  they are offered in the menu of kinds; those in the contents are in hand
  whether chosen here or not.
- **Kinds of paragraph and words**: those that belong to a document of its
  kind, as the parts of a script belong to a screenplay.
- **Requirements**: words of text, words of abstract, keywords, whether the
  names of the authors are left out, for review, and a note on what the
  limits count; *no limit* where nothing is said. The checklist and the
  preview count against these; nothing is cut.
- **Files**: the kinds of file the document is made as.

What you change is kept as a template of your own, under the name you give
it; those that came with Glaukopis stay as they were. **Delete this
template** takes one of your own away, and the elements that used it use
the template it was made from. A template is copied into the project when it
is chosen there, so that those you share the project with, and a
`.glaukopis` file, have it.

## The document settings

Every element has a window of **document settings**, for the document it
prints. **Ctrl+Shift+D** opens it for the element in view; so do **Document
settings…** in the menu of the element in view (**This element**, beside its
name in the bar) and the button over the preview. Down its left stand six
parts. The template, the layout, the reference style and the language take
effect as they are chosen, each a step of undo; the details are kept with
**Save**.

**Template** lists the templates by group, with a search by the kind of
text, a journal or a publisher. The one in use is marked, with where its
requirements are from, the layout and the reference style it brings, and
**Change this template…** and **New template…** (see *Templates*). A
template taken from an element over this one says **From** which; where
the element chose one itself, a button takes the one over it again, or your
usual.

**Details** is what stands on the first page, and what the files carry as
metadata: the **title**, which is the element's name until one is written,
and shows the name in its place meanwhile; the **subtitle**; the
**authors**, each with affiliation, e-mail and ORCID; the **date**, as it is
to be printed; the **keywords**, separated by commas, counted against what
the template allows; and the **identifiers**, ISBN, edition, publisher,
place and DOI, and any other you name, such as an ISSN. The authors, the
date, the keywords and the identifiers are the element's own where it sets
them, and otherwise taken from the nearest element over it that does, up to
the project itself: under such a setting the window says **From** which
element it comes, and the field shows the inherited value in grey, until
you write your own. The title and the subtitle are never taken from above:
they are each element's own. Everything else is written in the tree.

**Parts** lists the kinds of elements the template gives a place to, each
with where it stands, front matter, back matter or cover, and the elements
directly under this one that fill it, or **None yet**. **Make one** makes an
element of the kind under this one, named after the kind, first among them
for the front matter and last for the rest; pressing an element goes to it
in the text. Where a document has one of a kind and more are there, the
second and the later say **Only the first is used**.

**Look** names the layout and the reference style in use, and says where
they are chosen and changed: in Look, the panel beside the preview, which
**Open Look** opens (see *The look of a document*).

**Language** is the language of the text of this element, and of everything
under it that is given no other, for a part written in another language
than the rest. Where the element says none, it says **From** the element
over it that does, naming the language; where none does, the text is
written in the language of new texts, which is set under *Settings* ›
*Language*: at first that of the computer, where documents have words in it
(every language Glaukopis speaks), and English otherwise. On the project
itself it is **the language of the project**, what the project is written
in, for everyone it is shared with; a document brought in keeps the language
the document says it is in. The language is written into the project, so
that those you share it with make the same document of it. It decides what
the document prints in words of its own, the headings of the notes, the
abstract and the bibliography, what a figure and a table are called, and the
words of the reference style; the dictionary the spelling is checked by; and
the quotation marks a mention stands in. **Languages…**, in the menu of the
element in view, opens the window at this part.

**Checklist** is what the template requires, against the text and the tree;
nothing is cut. It says the words of the text against the limit, with the
template's note on what the limits count, and whether the authors are to be
left out, for review; then what there is to remark on: more words than the
template allows, more words in the abstract, more keywords, a second element
of a kind the document has one of, beside the first, and elements of the
kind **Cover** where the template makes no cover. Each names its elements,
which open in the text; otherwise it says **Nothing to remark on**.

## The preview, and the manuscript

**Ctrl+P** opens the preview beside the map, and closes it. It shows the
pages of the element in view, with all under it, as they will be, and follows
as you write, a moment behind: it waits until the writing pauses. Any
element is a document: enter a chapter, and the preview shows the chapter
alone, and exports it so. Of a long document the pages take a few seconds to be
made, and the preview waits the longer for it; the writing is not held up
meanwhile. The pages that are looked at are the ones that are drawn, and
the others as they are moved to.

The text on the pages can be selected and copied, as it is set: a passage
dragged over and copied with **Ctrl+C** comes out line by line, across
pages. Two buttons over the pages bring the text and the pages to the same
place. **Go to this place in the text** shows in the text the element whose
pages are in view; **Show where the text is** moves the pages to where the
element you are writing in begins, and points its page out for a moment.

One line over the preview names what the document is made by: the
template, the layout and the reference style in use. Pressing it opens
Look, where the look of the document is set (see *The look of a
document*); the button with the sliders, at the right, opens the document
settings (see *The document settings*).

The **layout** is the page, the type, the spacing, the headings, as a
publisher or a journal asks for them. The layouts that come with Glaukopis
each say where their requirements were found, and when. They are offered by
kind: the general ones, **fiction** (a novel as a manuscript for submission,
and as a book), **stage and screen** (a play script and a screenplay),
**poetry** (a collection of poems), and the style guides, publishers and
journals of academic writing. The **reference style** is how citations and
the bibliography are written: Chicago, MLA, APA, Harvard and other common
styles come with Glaukopis, and any of the ten thousand styles of the
Citation Style Language can be fetched by name. A project is not of one
kind: any template, layout or style can be given to any element, and
changed at any time.

Both are chosen, and the look changed, in Look (see *The look of a
document*).

**Export** makes the manuscript: PDF, Word, OpenDocument, LaTeX, Markdown, a
web page, or an **e-book** (EPUB), in which the reader sets the text and each
chapter is a part of its own. The cover of the e-book is the first picture
in an element of the kind **Cover** under the element printed (see *What
goes into the document*). Where the document is given as it is written, in
LaTeX, Typst or Markdown, its pictures are put in a folder beside it, named
after it.

In Word and Writer every kind is a style, with the look the layout gives
it, so that a publisher can change every epigraph at once: the paragraph
styles *Attribution*, *Epigraph*, *Scene Heading*, *Action*, *Character*,
*Dialogue*, *Parenthetical*, *Transition*, *Headword*, *Gloss*, *Source
Code* and *Section Break*; the character styles *Speaker*, *Stage
Direction*, *Foreign*, *Title of a Work*, *Term* and *Mention*; for a kind
of your own, a style of its name; and for each part of the document, an
abstract or an appendix, a paragraph style of its name as well. In every
document that is made, foreign words and a passage in another language
carry their language, a mention stands
in the quotation marks of the language of the text, code is kept letter for
letter, and a break prints the sign the layout gives it; a draft note and a
highlight go into none of them.
The numbers of the lines you number do not stand in the margin of Word,
which numbers lines only by the section: they stand at the head of their
lines, small, and the words in the margin of a line at its end. Reference
marks are raised there, whatever the layout says.

There are two kinds of PDF. **PDF** is what the preview shows. **PDF, set by
LaTeX** is the same document in the typesetting of LaTeX, for those who
prefer its pages or are asked for them; it needs LaTeX (TeX Live) on the
computer, and takes a little longer. The two are alike in everything the
layout says, and differ in how lines and pages are broken.

What there is to remark about a document, such as a font the layout asks for
and the computer does not have, is said at the foot of the preview, with
the words of the text against what the template allows.

Two programs do the work. Pandoc turns the text into the document, and sets
every citation and the bibliography in the reference style. Typst sets the
pages: those of the preview, and those of the PDF, which are the same.

## The look of a document

Everything that decides how the pages look is in one place: **Look**, a
panel at the side, beside the preview. It opens from the line over the
preview, from the foot of the kind menu, from the document settings, from
the palette, where **Change the font…** opens it at the type, and by
**Ctrl+Shift+A**. It is the look of the document the preview shows: of the
element in view, with all under it.

At its top one line says what makes the look: the **template**, what the
document consists of; the **layout**, how the page looks, with the number
of changes the document makes to it; and the **reference style**, how it
cites: *Plain document → Manuscript · 2 changes → Chicago*. Pressing the
template opens it in the document settings; pressing the changes lists
them, each with where it was made. Under the line the layout and the
reference style are chosen. The first choice of each is **As the template
has it**, naming what the template brings, or **From** the element over
this one that chose, naming what it chose; then come the layouts by kind,
and the reference styles by kind, with **More styles…**, which fetches any
of the ten thousand styles of the Citation Style Language by name. **Change
this reference style…** has the changes publishers most often ask for as
simple choices, and the whole style, part by part, for the rest; what you
change in it is kept as a style of your own.

A field under them finds any setting by its words: *font*, *margins*, *page
numbers*. Otherwise the settings stand in parts, which the buttons under
the field turn to: **Type** (the font, its size, the spacing, how italics
and reference marks are set), **Page** (its size, the margins, the
**Columns** and the **Gap between columns**, the page numbers and the
running head), **Paragraphs**, **Headings** (with whether a level of
heading **begins a new page**, as the chapters of a book do), **Kinds**,
**Quotations**, **Numbers**, **Notes**, **Bibliography**, **Figures and
tables**, **Title and abstract**, and **About the layout**, which says where
its requirements were found, and when.

Under **Kinds** every kind is listed: first those this document uses, then
the rest by group, and your own kinds after them, each with what it is
based on and, at the right of its line, what is said of it, or *as the
base* where nothing is. Open a kind, and a line says how it will stand,
**As it will stand**: the base look of the kind, then the kinds it is based
on, then what the layout and the document say, resolved into one. The rows
under it are size, alignment, indents, the first line, space before and
after, bold, italic, letters, underlining, letters of equal width, whether
it keeps with what follows, and whether it begins a new page; where a row
says nothing it shows what the base comes to, *As the base (1.27cm)*.
Under **Numbers**, how the numbers of lines you number are shown where they
do not say otherwise, **Show a number every** and **Numbers on the** left or
the right, and whether the lines of the page are numbered, as some journals
ask for review.

What you change in Look is the document's own: the layout stays as it is,
and the change is shown at once in the preview. Under a row you have
changed it says **Changed here**, and beside it **take Manuscript’s
again**, which takes the change away. The changes reach every element under
the one they are made on, as the language does: in a chapter, a change made
on the book says **Changed in “The book”**, and the chapter can change it
again for itself. The changes are kept with the project, and in its Org
form as the element's **LOOK**.

To use the same look elsewhere, **Keep these changes as a layout of my
own…** at the foot of Look asks for a name and makes a layout of the
layout with the changes in it, which the document then uses, with no
changes of its own; it is offered among your own layouts in every project.
Where the layout is already one of your own, **Put these changes into** it
does the same without a new name, for every document that uses it. A layout
of your own is deleted under **About the layout**. The layouts that come
with Glaukopis stay as they are.

## Working together

A project can be shared, so that several write in it at the same time. This
needs a server, which you or your institution runs: see `server.md`.

**To share a project**, open it and click the two figures at the top right.
Enter the address of the server, and the password of the server if it asks
for one. Then **Make an invitation code**, and send the code and the address
of the server to the one you invite. A code is shown only once, when it is
made; to invite someone later, make a new one.

**To join a project**, choose **Join a shared project** among the projects,
and enter the address and the code. An invitation that is pasted whole is
taken apart for you.

From then on:

- What one writes is seen by the others at once, with a cursor that carries
  the name of who is writing.
- Those who have the project open are shown at the top right.
- Each has the whole project on their own computer. Without the network, or
  without the server, the work goes on; what was written meanwhile is brought
  together when the server is reached again.
- Undo takes back what you wrote yourself, and leaves what others wrote.
- The one who shared the project can see who has it, remove someone, and stop
  sharing. Those who are removed keep the project as it was then.

The files of the library are not shared; the references themselves are.
The pictures of the figures of the project are shared: they are sent to the
server when they are put in, and fetched by the others from there, into
their own stores. No other picture of your store is sent. A picture that
has not arrived yet is shown as an empty frame until it has.

## The history of a project

A project can keep its whole history: every change, with who made it and
when. The project can then be looked at as it was at any moment, and what
stood there brought back. It is off at first, and turned on for one project
at a time.

**To open the history**, choose its tab in the panel at the side, or press
**Ctrl+Shift+H**. While the history is not kept, the panel says so and has one
button, **Keep the history**: from that moment the history begins with the
project as it is, and nothing written after it is thrown away. The setting
is in the project, so in a shared project the others keep the history too.

The panel lists the moments, the newest first:

- **What one person wrote before a pause**, with when they began and ended
  and how much was written and deleted. In a shared project each has the
  colour they have while they write.
- **Moments with a name**, marked with a bookmark.
- **The history begins**, at the bottom.

**Choosing a moment** shows the map as it was then, in the place of the map
as it is; it cannot be written in. What changed since the moment before is
marked in the colour of who changed it: what was added is underlined and lies
on their colour, what was removed is struck through, and what only had its
marks changed is underlined more faintly. **Back to the present** at the top
leaves it, and so does choosing the same moment again.

From a moment:

- **Name this moment**, the bookmark at the top. A named moment is easy to
  find again, is never merged away, and is seen by those you share the
  project with.
- **Bring this element back as it was**, the arrow that comes up beside the
  heading of an element; or **Bring the map back as it was**, the arrow at
  the top. What is brought back is a change of yours like any other: undo
  takes it back, and the others see it.
- **Open as a project of its own**: a new project, named after the day it
  was, which you can read and write in without touching this one.

### The room it takes, and taking some of it out

**Settings of the history**, at the top of the panel, shows how much room
the history takes, and holds:

- **Keep the history**, which turns it off again. What was kept is then
  deleted, after it has asked — in a shared project, on every computer. The
  project itself stays as it is.
- **Older history**, which is merged so that it takes less room and is read
  sooner: everything within an hour into one after some weeks, everything
  within a day into one after some months; four weeks and six months at
  first. Moments within what is merged can no longer be told apart. Named
  moments, and those a review compares with, are always kept. It is done
  once each time the project is opened.
- **What came before**: choose a moment in the list first, then open the
  settings, and the history before that moment can be put in a file of its
  own (**Archive…**) or **deleted**. What is left begins with the project as
  it was at that moment. Where named or reviewed moments lie before it, it
  says how many before it asks.

An archive is a file ending in `.glaukopis-history`. **Open an archive…**, at
the top of the panel, shows it in the place of the project's own history: the
moments in it can be looked at, and opened as projects of their own, though
not named or brought back. **Close** goes back to the history of the project.

The history is kept beside the project on your own computer, and holds the
project and all its changes: a project of two hundred thousand words takes
some megabytes when the history is turned on. Keeping it does not make
writing slower, and a project with a long history opens as quickly as one
without: the history is read only when you open the panel, and that takes
well under a second.

## Reviewing changes

What the others have written in a shared project can be gone through
afterwards, change by change, and accepted or rejected, much as in Word or
LibreOffice. Nobody's writing is held back meanwhile: what is written is in
the text at once, and the review comes after. It needs the history of the
project, which tells who changed what, and when; where the history is not
kept, the panel says so, and offers to keep it from then on.

**Changes**, in the panel at the side (**Ctrl+Shift+E**), is where the
changes of the element in view, and of all under it, are reviewed, beside
its text. It lists them in the order of the text, says how many are left,
and shows one at a time: where it is, as it was and as it is, with the words
that changed marked, each person's in their colour, and who made it and
when. While the panel is open, the text shows the changes where they are:
what was added under a tint of the colour of who added it, what was deleted
struck through, where it stood. Opening the panel leaves the view as it is;
going on to a change from the panel turns the pane to the text, where the
change you look at is brought into view, opened where it is folded away, and
marked more strongly.

What is one change:

- **A sentence**, with the words that changed marked in it, however many
  places in it changed, and however many people changed it. Formatting is
  part of its sentence.
- **New text** of a sentence or more, however long, and so **text deleted**:
  paragraphs written one after another are one change.
- **A paragraph moved**: deleted in one place and written, with nearly the
  same words, in another.
- **An element** added, deleted, moved, left out of the document or put back,
  or printed as a heading or not. An element added with its text is one
  change.
- **A figure, an equation or a table** put in or taken out, and a citation, a
  formula or a note put into a sentence where nothing else of it changed. The
  text of a note, of what is said of a figure, and of a cell is gone through
  sentence by sentence.

**By paragraph**, at the top of the panel, makes each paragraph that changed
one change: for a quick look at text much rewritten.

| To | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Accept the change: it is not shown again unless it changes again | **Accept**, or **Ctrl+Alt+Y** |
| Put the text back as it was | **Reject**, or **Ctrl+Alt+N** |
| Go on to the next, and leave this one for later | **Later**, or **F8** |
| Go back to the one before | **Shift+F8** |
| Go through the list | The arrows up and down, in the panel |

Rejecting is a change of yours like any other: the others see it, and
**Ctrl+Z** takes it back. It counts as reviewed, so what you rejected is
not put before you again. An element that was deleted, and a figure or a
table taken out, are brought back from the history instead.

You can write in the text while you review it. The change you write in is
worked out anew as you write, and **Accept** accepts it as it then stands;
what you wrote is yours, for the others to review.

**Its history**, under a change, shows every version between the two ends,
with who made it and when. **Accept up to here** accepts the versions up to
that one, and leaves those after it to be reviewed; **Use this version**
makes the text what it was then, and accepts it.

The review goes from where you last reviewed: what you have accepted is kept
in the project, so that it holds on every computer you work on. When nothing
is left that you have not accepted, anywhere in the project, the review
begins anew from then. Your first review goes from when the history began.
At the top of the panel another moment can be chosen to review from: a
session of the history, or a moment given a name. Your own changes are left
out, unless you ask for **Your own changes too**.

## Languages

Glaukopis speaks English; Norwegian Bokmål and Nynorsk, Swedish, Danish,
Finnish and Icelandic; German, Dutch, French, Italian, Spanish, and
Portuguese as Portugal and as Brazil write it; Romanian, Albanian and
Greek; Polish, Czech, Slovak, Slovenian, Croatian, Bosnian, and Serbian in
both its scripts; Ukrainian, Belarusian and Russian; Turkish; Hindi,
Marathi, Bengali, Gujarati, Tamil and Telugu; Chinese in simplified and in
traditional characters; and Japanese. English and Bokmål are the author's
own. The others were translated with the help of a language model, under a
glossary and mechanical checks, and have not yet all been read by people
who speak them: a word that is wrong or odd in your language is worth a
line to develop@robertemilberge.no. At first Glaukopis speaks the language
of the computer, where it has it, and English where it does not.
*Settings* › *Language* changes it, at once.

Your texts have a language of their own, which need not be that of
Glaukopis. It is chosen under **Language** in the document settings of an
element (**Ctrl+Shift+D**, or **Languages…** in the menu of the element in
view, which opens the settings there): that of the project, for everyone it
is shared with, and that of an element written in another language than the
rest, which holds for everything under it that is given no other. See *The
document settings*.

The language of an element decides what its document prints in words of its
own: the headings of the notes, the abstract and the bibliography, what a
figure and a table are called, and the words of the reference style, such as
"and", "p." and "edited by". A layout that has words of its own keeps them;
those it has as they are in English are printed in the language of the
text.

## The words of Glaukopis

The words of the interface in any language can be read through and mended
in Glaukopis itself. At the foot of *Settings*, **Make a translation project
of Glaukopis strings** makes a project, *Words:* and the language, with an element
for each part of the application, and under it an element for each message,
named by it.
In its text the English stands above, as a quotation, with what it says to
translators where it says anything; under it are the words of the language,
which are what you change. Spelling is checked in the language, search and
replace work as in any project, and the status of an element can say what
you have read.

The words are written as Fluent, as in the files: `{ $count }` is a number
the program puts in, and a choice by number stands on several lines. Keep
those as they are, and change the words around them.

**Write as Fluent files…**, in the bar over the project, writes what you
changed into the Fluent files of a folder: the folder of the language in a
copy of Glaukopis's code (`locales/nb`), or any folder, to send the files to
develop@robertemilberge.no. Only what you changed is written, and nothing
that was changed in the files since the project was made. What cannot be
written, because it has lost a variable of the English or a form of the
plural, or is no longer good Fluent, is listed, to be mended and written
again.

**Use them now**, beside it, puts what you changed into the words this
Glaukopis speaks, at once, without waiting for a new version: the menus and
dialogs show your words as soon as the button is pressed. They are kept in
the folder `words` of the data directory, and are used each time Glaukopis
starts; a word that no longer fits a newer version, having lost a variable
the English has, is left out, and the English one stands. The same part of
the settings says which languages have words you changed in use, and takes
them away again. Files of words from someone else work the same way: put them
in `words`, in a folder named by the language (`words/nb`), and start
Glaukopis.

## Spelling

Glaukopis checks the spelling of what you write as you write it, in the
language of the text: the text, the names of the elements, and the notes.
**Foreign words** and a passage given a **Language** (see *Kinds of
paragraph and of words*) are checked in their own language where there is a
dictionary for it, and left alone where there is none. A word that the dictionary does not know has a wavy line
under it, in the text you are writing and in the text that is only shown.
The word you are in the middle of writing is left alone until you leave it.

Right-click an underlined word, or press **F7**, which goes to the next one
after the cursor (**Shift+F7**, to the one before), and its menu has:

- **What it may be**, the likeliest first. What you choose takes the word's
  place. In Norwegian, whose words are long, it can take half a second to
  find.
- **Add to my words**: the word is right from then on, in all your projects,
  in the language of the text. A word added in English holds in American and
  British English alike.
- **Ignore in this project**: the word is left alone in this project, and
  for those you share it with. One written in small letters is left alone
  with a capital as well.

What is not checked: citations, formulas and the other things in the text
that are not words; loose citations in a text you brought in, which are
not yet citations; words with digits, such as *1990s*; addresses of web
pages and of mail; and words written in another script than the
dictionary's, such as Greek in an English text.

Dictionaries are imported under *Settings* › *Language* › *Spelling*.
**Import spelling dictionaries** lists those the server of Glaukopis offers:
English (American and British), Norwegian (Bokmål and Nynorsk), Swedish,
Danish, German, French and Italian, for now. **Import** fetches one, and it
is used at once; one that is newer on the server than the one you have can
be **Updated**, and **Close** hides the list again. In the list of
**Dictionary languages**, each says whether it is the system's or imported,
and **×** beside an imported one takes it away. Beside the address of the
server, **Change** gives another, or a folder on the computer, for packages
of your own or where there is no network; `docs/language-packages.md` tells
how a package is made.

A text in English without a country is checked with the American and the
British dictionary both, and a word is right if either has it. The
dictionaries of the computer are used as well: on Linux the packages named
`hunspell-…` put them in `/usr/share/hunspell`, and the packages of Glaukopis
for Debian, Ubuntu and Fedora bring those of English along. A dictionary you
have imported comes before the computer's of the same language. A dictionary
of your own can also be put, as its `.aff` and `.dic` file, in the folder
`dictionaries` of the data directory, which is shown under *Settings*; it
comes before all the others of its language. Where there is no dictionary
for the language of the text, nothing in it is underlined, and **F7** says
so.

*Settings* › *Language* › *Spelling* turns the checking off and on, and lists
the dictionaries there are and where they came from. **Manage words added to
dictionaries** shows the words you have added, a language at a time, where
they can be taken away and others added. The last button of the tools over the text
turns the checking off and on as well; it is pressed in while spelling is
checked.

The Norwegian dictionaries are made from the word lists of Bokmålsordboka
and Nynorskordboka (Universitetet i Bergen og Språkrådet, ordbøkene.no,
CC-BY 4.0), with every form of every word, and the rules of spell-norwegian
that put words together and make the genitive. They are large, and are read
the first time a text in Norwegian is checked: the first underlines of a
session come after a moment.

## Colours

Under *Settings* › *Appearance* the colours of the interface are chosen:
**Light**, **Dark**, **Mellow**, pastel and warm, with the contrast kept
where text is read, or **As the system**, light or dark as the computer is.
**Your own** makes a colouring from four colours you choose: the paper, the
ink, the accent that marks what is chosen and pressed, and the second voice
that marks associations and comments; everything else follows from these,
and whether the scheme is light or dark follows from the paper. Begin from
one of the themes and change what you will; you are warned where the ink or
the accent would be hard to read on the paper. In the text, the sheet lies
on a desk of another shade, so that where the text goes is seen.

## Keeping things safe

- **Earlier versions.** A version of each project is kept every now and then
  while you work. Right-click a project and choose **Earlier versions…** to
  open one as a project of its own.
- **The whole history.** A project can keep every change, with who made it
  and when: see *The history of a project*.
- **Deleted projects** can be brought back, from the line under the projects.
- **A project as one file.** Right-click a project, on the home page or in the
  list of projects, and choose **Save as a file…**: a `.glaukopis` file holds
  the project, its comments, the pictures its figures show and the works it
  cites, to keep, to move to another computer, or to send. **Open a project
  file…**, on the home page and at the head of the list of projects, makes a
  new project of one, never in the place of one you have. The works it cites
  are compared with your library: those that are the same need nothing, new
  ones are added, and where the file's differs from yours, or looks like a
  work you have, you choose, in one window, which to keep; the citations follow
  your choice. The history of the project, and the files of the library, do
  not go with it.
- **Everything is in one folder**, shown under *Settings*. To keep a copy of
  your work, copy the folder. In it, `library` holds the references
  (`library.bib`), the files of the library (`attachments`, each under a
  name made of what it holds) and `files.json`, which says what each file is
  called and which folder it is in; **Export all files…** gives a copy you
  can read in any file manager.
- **What Glaukopis did is written down.** Each run keeps a log in the folder
  `logs`, beside the rest, and the last ten are kept: what was done, what
  went wrong, and what became of the window. **The log**, under *Settings*,
  opens the folder. When something has gone wrong, such as a window that
  closed, the log of that run is what to look at, or to send.
- **To read your text without Glaukopis**, export it, or open its folder:
  every project is kept there as Org as well (see below). The references are
  in `library.bib` at all times, which other tools can read.

### Your project as Org

Every project is also kept as plain text that you, and other programs, can
read without Glaukopis. Its folder, under *projects* in the folder shown
under *Settings* (each named by the letters and digits Glaukopis knows the
project by; its `project.org` begins with the project's name), holds beside
what Glaukopis works from:

- `project.org`: the whole project in Org, the plain text form of Org mode.
  Each element is a headline at its depth in the tree, the project first;
  its text is under it, with its kinds of paragraph as Org's blocks
  (`#+BEGIN_QUOTE`, `#+BEGIN_VERSE`, lists with `- `), its marks as Org's
  (`/italic/`, `*bold*`), its notes as `[fn::…]` and its citations as
  `[cite:@key]`. What is said of an element (its kind, how far it has come,
  when it is, its associations, its settings as a document) is in a drawer
  under its headline, and its comments in another.
- `references.bib`: the works the project cites, by the keys the citations
  use.
- `pictures/`: the pictures its figures show.

It is written anew a few seconds after the project is saved, and Glaukopis
never reads it back: it cannot get your project wrong, and nothing is lost
if you change it or delete it. To have it somewhere else, right-click a
project, on the home page or in the list of projects, and choose **Save as
Org…**, and choose a folder: the same three are written into it.

**Open an Org file…**, on the home page, makes a new project of an Org file,
or of a folder that holds `project.org`, as a project file does: its works
are compared with your library, and you choose where they differ. A file
written by Glaukopis comes back as the project it was; one you wrote
yourself, or one from Org mode, is read as well as it can be, and what has
no place in a project is kept as a draft paragraph, so that nothing is lost.

**The clipboard** speaks Org too. What you copy is, as plain text, its Org
form, so that a quotation or a list keeps its shape when it is pasted into a
plain text editor; text pasted that is written in Org (a block, a list, a
citation) comes in with its kinds and marks. Text that is not Org is pasted
as before, line by line.

**The history and the review** can show what changed as Org: tick **Show as
Org** to see the text as it was and as it is, the lines taken out and put in
marked, and within a changed line the words that changed.

`project.org` is read by Org mode in Emacs, with its outline, folding and
tables, and by Pandoc, which turns it into many other forms; any editor of
plain text shows it as it is.

## What Glaukopis needs

On Windows and macOS, Pandoc and Tesseract come with Glaukopis, Tesseract
with the data for reading English that it has of its own, and so do the
fonts that come with Typst. On Linux these come from the packages of the
system instead: on Arch, Pandoc and the font Libertinus are installed with
Glaukopis, and the rest is optional, said by the package. The packages for
Debian, Ubuntu and Fedora stand between: Pandoc and the fonts come with
them, and Tesseract and the dictionaries of English are recommended from
the system's packages, so that `apt` and `dnf` install them along. On every
system the dictionaries of spelling and the data for reading other
languages are imported under *Settings* (see *Spelling* and *Text in scans
and pictures*), and on Linux those of the system's packages serve as well.

The installers for Windows and macOS are not signed. On Windows, the
installer is let run with **More info** › **Run anyway**. On macOS, open
Glaukopis the first time by right-clicking it in *Applications* and choosing
**Open**; if macOS says it is damaged, run `xattr -cr
/Applications/Glaukopis.app` in the Terminal and try again.

Pandoc makes the documents: Pandoc 3.1.10 or newer. One that is older is said
to be so under *Settings*. It is installed with Glaukopis when Glaukopis is
installed as a package, and where it is found is shown under *Settings*.
Typst, which sets the pages of the preview and of the PDF, is part of
Glaukopis; it sets with the fonts of the computer, and on Windows and macOS
with those it brings itself as well. On Arch the same fonts are packages:
`otf-libertinus`, the serif it falls back on and the font mathematics is set
in, is installed with Glaukopis, and `ttf-dejavu`, for code, and
`otf-latin-modern`, for the font of LaTeX, are optional.

LaTeX is needed only for the PDF that is set by it. LuaLaTeX is used where
it is installed, since it knows the fonts of the computer and can turn to
another font for letters that the font of the document lacks, such as Greek
with its accents.

A drawing (SVG) in a PDF set by LaTeX, or in a Word document, is made into
what those can hold by `rsvg-convert`, which comes with librsvg and is on
most computers.

Tesseract reads the text of scans and pictures. On Arch it is optional:
the package `tesseract`, with `tesseract-data-eng` for English; the data of
other languages are packages of their own, such as `tesseract-data-nor` for
Norwegian, `tesseract-data-grc` for Ancient Greek and `tesseract-data-lat`
for Latin. Without it, nothing is read, and *Settings* says it is not found.
Where it is found, and which languages it reads, is shown
under *Settings*, where you can also choose the languages to read in at
first. The pages of a PDF that cannot be drawn otherwise, such as some that
are locked, are drawn by `pdftoppm`, which comes with Poppler, where it is
installed.
